# Bumpp Audience Intelligence Bible

**Public research edition · 21 November 2026**

An audience reference for developing dramas filmed in Roblox and reviewing finished shows across five cohorts. The audience includes viewers who follow Minecraft stories, social players and viewers who enjoy the genre without playing either game.

## Audience mandate

**Make the first shows for Lore Keepers:** US viewers, especially girls aged 16–19, who already choose the next installment of a Roblox story because they care what happens between its characters. Write for the viewer who recognizes a change in a friendship before either friend admits it. The creative test is whether a new cast can earn that degree of attention. A girl who follows a Minecraft alliance may be a closer prospect than a daily Roblox player who watches only building tutorials.

| Operating decision | Default for the engine |
|---|---|
| Core and age edges | Write first for 16–19. Plan for 20–24 as an older audience and 13–15 as younger viewers who may encounter the work. Give 17–21-year-old protagonists credible school, family, college or work lives. Default romance-led protagonists to 18+. The original 14–18 sweet-spot hypothesis remains open. |
| Markets | The US is the first creative and research market. APAC is the proposed next region. Begin comparative work in the Philippines, with the viewing language specified for each version. Treat Japan, Indonesia and other markets individually. |
| Medium | The show is filmed inside Roblox. A viewer can discover and watch it off-platform. Minecraft supplies a related storytelling tradition, a source of prospective viewers and a test of whether a world rule travels. A Roblox account, in-game spending and identifying as a gamer are optional. |
| Priority | Tier 1 carries the most weight in early development. Each show has exactly one primary cohort. A secondary cohort can benefit from an adaptation while the primary audience’s reason to return remains intact. |
| Outcome | A viewer chooses a chapter, follows the decisive action, and has a specific reason to seek another. A show review can judge whether the work supports that outcome. Only viewer behavior can establish whether it happened. |

### How to use this file

1. **Developing a show.** Start with the cohort directory, then read the *whole* primary profile. Complete the audience brief in [Development brief](#development-brief) before choosing the premise. Use the character, media, language and market chapters to make concrete decisions. Use one unnumbered viewing habit alongside the cohort if helpful.
2. **Reviewing a finished show.** Retrieve its original audience brief. Review the supplied work in [Finished-show audit](#finished-show-audit) against that cohort’s promises, including the specialist tests. Record evidence by episode and timestamp. Report the primary verdict and a five-cohort resonance matrix, including credible appeal to the other four cohorts.
3. **Interpreting evidence.** **Decision** means what Bumpp chooses to make. **Observation** means a published finding or an inspected work with a stated scope. **Hypothesis** means a proposed relationship between the work and audience response. **Unknown** means the required audience evidence is missing. The five profiles, examples and format suggestions are hypotheses unless a sentence identifies a source. All people and fragments of dialogue used for illustration are composites or originals.
4. **Navigating the reference.** After the five profiles, consult [market and consumer evidence](#market-and-consumer-evidence), [shows and media](#shows-and-media), [US and international settings](#us-and-international-settings), [voice and culture](#voice-and-culture), [story and format](#story-and-format), and [research and limitations](#research-and-limitations). This document contains both operating modes in full.

## Cohort directory

These are five widening editorial audiences, ordered by fit with the initial ambition. The first already loves the medium and a recurring cast. The short first-person lines below express editorial hypotheses about the viewing motive. The next three add people who enter through relationship drama, horror, or play. The last extends to someone who needs only a human story. An individual can like all five. Choose a show's primary by the pleasure its creators promise and protect when two choices conflict. Secondary interests do not change its identification. Tier order is a commissioning decision, and audience shares are unknown.

| Primary cohort | The viewer’s reason to choose another part | The distinction that changes the writing |
|---|---|---|
| [Tier 1 · Lore Keepers](#tier-1--lore-keepers) | “I know what that meant *between those two*.” | Accumulated relationship history changes the meaning of a present action. Give the best friend a continuing life. |
| [Tier 2 · Drama Girls](#tier-2--drama-girls) | “I need to see which one will actually choose the other.” | Familiar relationship tension can draw them to unfamiliar avatars. Earn a reversal through agency on both sides. |
| [Tier 3 · Scream Team](#tier-3--scream-team) | “I saw it change. Who else knows?” | An observable rule gives an individual or a group the pleasure of fear, prediction and release. |
| [Tier 4 · The Lobby](#tier-4--the-lobby) | “That’s our teammate. I want to see how they get out of this.” | A legible challenge or joke opens the door. A choice with a social cost makes watching worth time away from play. |
| [Tier 5 · The Crossover](#tier-5--the-crossover) | “My roommate would watch this even if I never mentioned Roblox.” | A genre or real-life stake must work for a newcomer on first contact. |

**Choosing between neighbors.** A mystery about a missing teammate is **Lore Keepers** if prior friendship makes a witness’s omission hurt, **Scream Team** if working out the rule is the main pleasure, or **The Lobby** if the group has to decide who gives up the winning position. For a relationship-led premise, ask whether knowing this particular cast is the draw (Lore Keepers) or whether someone will cross from another medium for this pairing or trope (Drama Girls). If the premise survives chiefly on a recognizable dilemma and the recommendation of a friend, consider The Crossover. In a brief, name the one payoff the show would protect if the runtime were cut in half.

**Viewing habits to record in any cohort.** Returning Roblox-series viewer, Minecraft or other serialized-world viewer, primarily a player, clip-first viewer, and genre-first newcomer describe the route into a show. These have no tier numbers. For example, “Scream Team · Minecraft-first · watches with a cousin” tells writers more than a demographic label and leaves room for a Roblox-native member of the same cohort.

## Tier 1 · Lore Keepers

**Primary audience for the engine.** This viewer follows a particular *cast* across Roblox episodes. Ask what changed between two people last time and which installment they deliberately sought next. Their answer might be about the lead’s oldest friend, a rival who kept a promise, or two characters who used to sit together. That remembered relationship is the basis for writing a show for them. A large edit account is one possible expression of the habit. Quiet, persistent viewing is just as valuable.

**Recruit first.** US 16–19-year-olds who can name a Roblox series they returned to unprompted. Prioritize girls in the initial group and include other genders with the same viewing history. Compare 13–15 and 20–24, city and small-town life, paid and free-only viewers, and US English- and Spanish-first viewers. In the Philippines, start with the same return behavior in the respondent’s preferred language. The sample needs both returning viewers and people who dropped a similar show at episode one.

### The person and their week

Picture a 17-year-old in a US suburb. On Tuesday she has rehearsal, her closest friend has a shift, and by the time they can talk neither has time for a whole episode. She saves it until Wednesday because the debrief is half the pleasure. At school they disagree about whether someone who stayed friends with both sides after a breakup was being kind or cowardly. A fictional version gives them a safer way to have that argument. She also follows a creator whose out-of-character interviews interest her more than some of the episodes. A recommendation from that creator could win the first click. The new cast still has to earn the second.

Her everyday brands might include YouTube on a family TV, Spotify on the trip home and a free editor such as CapCut. One friend likes building outfits in Berry Avenue, one likes drawing the cast, and another only texts a timestamp. A birthday Robux balance, an occasional snack after rehearsal and a pair of shoes she is saving for are three different relationships to money. On an ordinary week, she may spend nothing. A working 19-year-old who watches in private and never posts is an equally plausible Lore Keeper. Both portraits are illustrative.

| Decision for the writer | What to build or look for |
|---|---|
| What they consume | Start with a character-driven Roblox series such as *Walden Academy*, *Strings Attached* or *Royal Assistant*. Add cast debriefs, character edits, alternate viewpoints and game-world roleplay. Ask what they finished. Someone who only watches how to recreate an outfit has a different reason for being there. |
| What they want to feel | The pleasure of knowing someone well enough to spot a lie, belonging to the group that remembers why it matters, and having room to change sides. A comfortable routine can make a small rupture devastating. |
| Genres with a reason to work | Found family, friendship mystery, slow-burn rivalry, status drama, competition for a place in a group. A school, studio, team or first workplace gives people a reason to keep meeting after a falling-out. An inherited seat at the table can matter as much as inherited wealth. |
| Character network | Give the lead a useful skill and one person they cannot easily disappoint. Give the best friend a goal that could take them out of the lead’s orbit. Give the rival a true observation about the lead. Write down who knows each secret and who merely suspects. |
| How they speak and what they share | Close friends can communicate with a callback and half a sentence. A character who jokes in front of the group may become exacting in private. The shareable moment might be a hand hovering above an empty chair. The private message can be as simple as “She still saved it.” |
| What breaks the contract | A character switches allegiance because the plot needs a twist. A reveal changes a past fact without accounting for the original witness. Everyone agrees which person is wrong. A constant run of public humiliation leaves no evidence of why the friends liked one another. |

**Format and route in.** A full episode or ordered playlist supports deliberate viewing. A short can introduce one debatable choice and give the viewer a clear way back to that episode. Test a compact chapter against a longer ensemble installment. Let a quiet exchange finish. Possible fan behavior includes rewatching, making an edit or simply having something to tell a friend tomorrow. The story must deliver its payoff for all three.

### Inside the core fandom

The same episode has to make sense to a committed fan who remembers the pilot and a friend they persuade to watch today. Keep a private continuity record for each major relationship. Track what both people used to do together, who first stopped doing it, who noticed, what the friend group thinks happened, and the favor neither person has repaid. Let the returning fan notice one thing before the characters do. Give the newcomer enough evidence to follow the present choice without a recap of every prior episode.

| Version of the core viewer | Where to look for the pleasure | What would give a false impression of demand |
|---|---|---|
| The quiet regular | Watches on their own, remembers old alliances, may never like or post. A small act of repair changes their view of someone. | Counting active commenters as the entire audience. |
| The friend who keeps the conversation going | Saves the cut to watch together, shares one line privately, or waits until school the next day to ask whose side a friend takes. | Assuming that an absence of public shares means the scene had no social life. |
| The editor or theorist | Looks for an image, line reading or contradiction that acquires meaning out of order. The original chapter must also work in sequence. | Treating a popular song, attractive avatar or isolated reaction clip as proof of loyalty to the original series. |
| The cast follower or aspiring maker | Enjoys rehearsals, performances or seeing how the set was made. May move to a different title with a performer. | Treating audition interest, a tutorial click or interest in a specific creator as transfer to an unknown cast. |

**What to keep if a cut runs long.** Preserve the earlier look, favor or joke that makes the final choice intelligible. A recap can compress logistics. It rarely replaces the pleasure of watching two people enjoy each other before something goes wrong. If a side character's absence would cost the group nothing, give that person an independent stake before adding another admirer or rival.

**Finished-show test.** Find the first moment of ordinary affection. Then find a later choice whose cost depends on having seen it. Identify who witnessed it, what each person thinks happened and what the choice changes at their next encounter. One timecoded beat should support a plausible defense of the least sympathetic character. The premiere should settle one local question while leaving a specific cast relationship in motion. If the closing question could apply to any cast, revise the relationship before adjusting the thumbnail.

**Evidence and uncertainty.** The project reviewed available transcripts of two *Walden* episodes, *Strings Attached* episode one and *Royal Assistant* episode one. Twenty Top-sorted *Walden* comments included reactions to specific jokes, theories and roleplay. This is the closest evidence in the file to the intended format. It supports designing for specific exchanges and interpretive pleasure. It leaves age, country, gender mix, silent viewing and transfer to an unfamiliar cast unresolved. [Timestamped reference analysis](02-reference-mechanics.md) · [Comment sample and coding limits](06-walden-comment-observations.json)

**A local variation.** A Philippine viewer might send an English-language Roblox scene to a Filipino-speaking cousin and discuss the motive in their shared language. Ask which titles, actors and phrases the pair actually use. The memory of a character’s past choice should survive a change of subtitle language.

**Release-night participant.** Imagine a second viewer who likes making a character edit or seeing a behind-the-scenes cast read. They might play Berry Avenue to restage a favorite pose, try a Dress to Impress challenge with a friend, listen to an album while editing, or borrow an outfit idea without buying it. Ask whether they would follow the next episode with unfamiliar performers and without the song. That separates affection for the story from admiration for a performer, the game or the edit.

**Story territory.** Compare a scholarship outsider who discovers the rules are different for a friend, a leaked cast list that exposes a private favor, a rival protecting the person who beat them, and two best friends with incompatible futures. A friendship can carry a whole season if both people have somewhere else they could go. Invite a reviewer to identify the smallest moment fans might remember six episodes later.

### Resonance scorecard · Lore Keepers

| # | Resonance signal | What "2" looks like in the finished show |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Named relationship in motion by the end of Episode 1 | A specific pair (ship, rivalry, or friendship) whose state has visibly changed |
| 2 | Ordinary affection established before rupture | A shared ritual, joke, or habit shown before anything goes wrong |
| 3 | Callback that changes meaning | An earlier look, object, or line returns later and means something different |
| 4 | Witness logic | We know who saw what, and a third person can reasonably misread it |
| 5 | Best friend or side character with their own goal | A secondary character makes a choice that is not about the lead |
| 6 | A defensible least-sympathetic character | One beat lets a fan argue for the "villain" |
| 7 | Theory hook | A planted, fair clue or unanswered question fans can debate |
| 8 | Screenshot and edit moments | The episode offers three distinctive moments a viewer could recall, save or send. For a standalone short, use one and document its scope |
| 9 | Consequences carry forward | The cost of a choice is still visible in the next episode |
| 10 | Authentic register | Dialogue and on-screen texts pass a local read-aloud and the listener-specific checks in [Voice and culture](#voice-and-culture) |

## Tier 2 · Drama Girls

**Qualifying behavior.** They seek out a relationship premise across formats. A feud, second chance, false relationship or interrupted friendship catches their attention before they recognize the actors or game. This tier adds people who have never deliberately watched Roblox fiction. “Drama Girls” preserves the bible’s name and female-first commissioning emphasis. The behavioral definition includes anyone who repeatedly makes this choice.

**Recruit first.** US 16–19-year-olds who followed a relationship story from an edit, recommendation or written chapter to another installment. Prioritize girls and young women, include same-habit viewers of other genders, and add 20–24-year-old workers and students. A viewer who never plays either game is especially informative here. In the Philippines, compare viewers coming from locally produced romance with those coming from imported dramas. Learn which specific relationship held them after discovery.

### The person and their week

Picture an 18-year-old in a US community college who lives at home and works two evenings. They see their closest school friend less often now. They still trade clips at lunch and have a running disagreement about which character deserves an apology. They might follow a webtoon on a phone, know one K-drama through edits alone, and save a full series to watch with a cousin. They recognize the *second-lead* conversation even when they prefer the story about two friends. On weekends it could be dance practice, a part-time shift, a group meal or making a playlist for the bus. Put one of those commitments on screen and show which one the protagonist would actually give up.

A family subscription, a free webcomic, a saved ReelShort or DramaBox clip, and a cinema ticket bought with shift money can coexist. They may browse Pinterest for rooms, Spotify for a song from an edit, or a clothing or beauty brand for an admired look. Admiration, household access and individual purchase are different facts. For a Philippine comparison, ask who first sent the story, what language the friends use to argue about it, and whether they followed the cast, the chapter or the pairing.

| Decision for the writer | What to build or look for |
|---|---|
| Competing titles and habits | Use *The Summer I Turned Pretty*, *Heartstopper* and *Maxton Hall* to distinguish choosing sides, enjoying tenderness and following class conflict. In the Philippines, *He’s Into Her* supplies a local school-rivalry comparison. Ask what the viewer abandoned, which edit they saved, and which complete story they chose. |
| Emotional promise and genres | Anticipation, reciprocal recognition and justice for someone underestimated. Try romantic rivalry, reconciliation, revenge with a cost, workplace competition or friendship after a betrayal. Two people who already love each other can still want incompatible futures. |
| Character network | Give each lead a goal achievable without the other. A rival can be right about the unfair selection process. A best friend can refuse to cover for the protagonist and remain sympathetic. In a pretend relationship, give both parties a credible way to leave. |
| Voice and shareability | A fan might call something a slow burn. The character inside it might remember the time the other person takes the late bus. Let the text to a friend be bolder than what is said face to face. A pause before publicly taking someone’s side can be the moment worth saving. |
| Abandonment and substitutes | A humiliating prank sold as affection, a hollow rich-person caricature or a lead who loses every ambition on falling in love. The alternatives include another subtitled serial, a webtoon with earned payoffs, or the conversation about the clip itself. |

**Format and route in.** Compare a short standalone scene with the longer sequence that gives its reversal meaning. A useful clip can answer one question immediately, such as whether someone keeps their word in public. The next chapter can ask what that promise cost in private. Make the full title and episode order findable when someone enters midway through a feed.

**Finished-show test.** Locate a gesture whose meaning changes between its first and second appearance. Identify each lead’s independent objective and a later choice affected by the other. Check that the friend has agency after the pairing forms. Ask a cold viewer to retell the relationship using ordinary words after watching with captions. If they can name the trope but cannot say why these particular people care, the romance or friendship still needs work.

**A local variation.** A US Spanish-first viewer might arrive via a Spanish character edit, a Manila viewer via a homegrown ensemble, and either via a Korean series. Give a translator the public meaning of a line and the private feeling it conceals. A literal declaration can ruin a scene built around restraint.

**The trope reader.** Imagine a 19-year-old who searches by “second chance” or “rivals to lovers,” follows a webtoon and saves a costume or room reference on Pinterest. A second viewer may know the lead through a single edit and never recognize the trope label. Test whether the same chapter gives the reader the anticipated emotional turn and lets the newcomer work out the attraction. Ask for a complete title they chose after the edit, the one they abandoned, and who first recommended it.

**Story territory.** A fake relationship gives both characters a public obligation. A transfer student who already knows the selection system can unsettle the apparent expert. A best friend can refuse to keep a secret and still be the person the lead calls first. Compare a glamorous workplace with an ordinary one and trace where skill or need changes the balance of power.

## Tier 3 · Scream Team

**Qualifying behavior.** They choose controlled fear, a mystery they can solve or a scare they want to experience with someone else. The person who pauses a video to inspect a door and the person who watches behind a cousin’s shoulder can love the same sequence for different reasons.

**Recruit first.** US 16–19-year-olds across genders who have chosen a second scary story, theory video or horror episode. Include 13–15-year-olds for intensity and clarity feedback, and 20–24-year-old horror fans for crossover. Compare solo, sibling and friend-group viewing, plus players who avoid watching horror. In the Philippines recruit one local-language group by actual recent titles. Fear tolerance and appetite for spoilers are individual questions.

### The person and their week

Picture a 17-year-old in a US town who plays cooperative horror with two school friends on Friday. One wants to finish the run, one wants to be scared together, and one would rather watch the replay. Between school, pickup basketball and a weekend shift, a long theory video is something to save for a free evening. A short promising a single visible anomaly is easier to watch on the bus. With a cousin, the conversation might be about which exit they would have used. With friends, it might be about why the bravest player left someone behind. They can be a horror fan who dislikes gore, avoids jump scares in headphones, or never plays scary games alone.

One viewer might choose a Blumhouse trailer, a Kane Pixels upload or a Roblox horror playthrough on YouTube before paying for a film. Possible spending includes a shared cinema trip, food on the way home, saving toward headphones or no discretionary purchase that week. Roblox, Minecraft, YouTube horror channels and a cinema chain occupy different parts of this life. For a Philippine comparison, ask which local thriller, narrated story or creator they would actually choose. ## Voice and culture

**A voice begins with the listener.** A teen who jokes through a mistake with a teammate may become painstakingly polite in front of a teacher, then leave a close friend's message unanswered. **Refresh procedure.** Before using a meme as the premise, log the date, market, language, channel, speaker, apparent audience and communicative purpose. Find a second independent example and a local reader who can say where it would sound wrong. Ask separately whether they *recognize*, *use* and *still enjoy* it. ### Humor and emotional escalation

Use the bible’s different paths from joking to sincerity as options for particular characters. A close friend who habitually deflects might try a joke in the group, open and close a private message, then say one precise thing when the ordinary joke fails. A more direct character may set a boundary immediately. Let the people in the scene establish what a late reply, a period or silence means between them.

| Device | Dramatic use | Check in the edit |
|---|---|---|
| Deadpan understatement | A practical comment buys time while fear rises. | Does a later change in cadence tell us when the character believes the threat? |
| Escalation | Two people take an absurd team problem increasingly seriously. | Can a viewer follow the logic of the escalation without knowing the meme? |
| Affectionate teasing | Someone uses a specific shared memory to let a friend save face. | Is the friendship evident before the tease? Who gets to answer back? |
| Self-mockery | The person who always cancels a plan admits it first. | Does the admission cost something when the friend actually needed them? |
| A trope used knowingly | A fan recognizes the familiar pairing before the people involved do. | Would the awkward relationship still be funny if the reference dated? |
| The joke that stops | A person usually quick with a comeback starts giving short, exact directions. | Can a viewer feel fear through the shift in behavior? |

**Read-aloud check.** Name the listener, the speaker’s immediate objective and the phrase either person would avoid in front of the group. Swap two characters’ lines and listen for what breaks. Read the screen text at phone size. Ask a local reader whether they recognize a phrase, use it themselves and still enjoy it. Record any unanswered check as unassessed.

### Public, private and character voice

A channel description has to sound trustworthy. A discovery caption has to tell a stranger what just changed. Characters speak to people they know and can leave half the thought unsaid. Keep those three voices separate. For the same incident, a caption could read *Someone removed her name before the judging started*. A teammate in a crowded lobby could say “Check the credits again.” In a private message that night, the same teammate could say “I saw it before you did.” A pause or a changed subject can carry more than a topical phrase. These lines are invented craft examples.

| Situation | Direction for the writer | What a local reader should be able to tell |
|---|---|---|
| A group thread goes quiet | Establish who usually answers immediately. Let a read receipt or the missing reply change this particular relationship. If the interface vanishes, a later choice should still reveal the consequence. | Who noticed the silence, who else could see it, and why answering publicly would cost more. |
| Friends deflect embarrassment | Use a callback to a previous failure, a deliberately literal answer or an interruption. Let one friend laugh and the other carry on working. | Whether the joke is affection, avoidance or an attempt to win the room. |
| A player switches audience | RP means roleplay, OOC means out of character, SMP refers to survival multiplayer, and griefing describes deliberately spoiling another player’s work or play. A character might use the term in a server and describe what happened plainly to a sibling. | Which listener already knows the term. Distinguish a character’s accusation from what the audience actually saw. |
| A fan discusses a clue | A headcanon is a fan’s interpretation, a ship is a hoped-for pairing, and canon refers to what the work establishes. Two equally invested fans can disagree about the motive while agreeing about the event. | What was on screen, what the fan supplied and what later evidence could change their mind. |
| Adults or multilingual friends enter | Give an adult their own vocabulary of work, worry and humor. Let a multilingual reader choose where a switch of language signals closeness, privacy or formality. Read once with captions alone and once aloud. | Whether the relationship stays legible in both versions and whether a character sounds like someone from the named place. |

Record country, community, speaker, listener, medium and observation date when proposing slang. A term on a US creator’s public caption may sound wrong in a quiet apology, or among friends in Manila. Terms associated with Black speech, queer communities and other specific communities have histories and speakers. Give a character a credible social setting before assigning them someone else’s whole register. Test ordinary versions alongside topical ones and invite a local reader to strike lines they would never send.

## Story and format

**Character card.** For each recurring character, write down a concrete want, a practical skill, an obligation outside the group, one person whose opinion matters, one thing they misread, and how they try to repair harm. Add something they do for fun with no plot benefit. Give each a private, public and pressured way of communicating. A shy protagonist can be decisive with a teammate. A popular one may be generous in private and timid in public. Let disabled, queer, multilingual and culturally specific characters have ambitions and comic range across genres. UCLA's 2026 gaming work identifies representation gaps reported by respondents. No single character should carry the burden of representing a whole group. [CSS gaming study](https://www.scholarsandstorytellers.com/2026-gaming-pull-out)

| Genre | Feeling to deliver | Device that works inside a game world | Reason to return |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friendship or romance | I see why either person might feel betrayed | Group access, a shared routine, a choice visible to peers | A favor that changes who owes whom |
| Horror or mystery | I spotted something they missed | A familiar space with one repeatable anomaly, a clue legible without audio | A fair revelation that changes whom the group trusts |
| Team or competition | I know why giving up the winning role costs this person | Clear rules, visible effort, one unequal starting condition | A rival becomes necessary for the next challenge |
| Ensemble comedy or slice of life | I would want to spend time in this place | Everyday avatar blocking, a repeated task, conflicting levels of expertise | A recurring joke acquires a different meaning after a change in the group |
| Fantasy or adventure | This impossible rule makes an ordinary decision harder | One visibly demonstrated world rule with a cost and an exception | The consequence persists in the world after the spectacle ends |
| Reality-style or performance drama | I understand the gap between what someone said publicly and what happened | Rehearsal, selection, a confessional or two perspectives on a shared task | Someone must live with a decision made for an audience |

**Format choices to compare.** For a first-contact mobile clip, test a 30–60-second complete dilemma with a visible payoff. For a short chapter, try 90 seconds to three minutes, long enough for an action, interpretation and consequence. For a deliberate viewing session, compare a connected sequence with room for quiet scenes. These are proposed test lengths. The roughly 38- and 39-minute *Walden* references show actual long-form supply in the same creative neighborhood. Their durations tell us nothing about ideal micro-episode length. Watch the complete clip muted, once, on a small display. Can the viewer identify the two people in conflict, the object or decision, and what changed? [Reference episode analysis](02-reference-mechanics.md)

**Roblox filming, Minecraft crossover.** Decide whether characters know they are players, live fully inside the fiction, or switch in a way the viewer can follow. Write that choice at the top of the concept. Maintain a short continuity record of permissions, objects, positions and who saw what. Give important figures distinguishable silhouettes, readable gestures and consistent staging. If two heads face the same direction, make the decisive choice visible through who steps aside or who takes the last seat. For Minecraft fans, let building, persistence, teamwork or perspective carry over. Transplanting named mobs, proprietary worlds or creator personas is unnecessary. For newcomers, show the outcome of the rule before asking them to remember its name.

**Age and authenticity check.** The 16–19 creative core can handle conflicting loyalties, fear, grief, complicated crushes and real consequences. Use suspense, implication and aftermath where an explicit depiction would overwhelm the relationship. Keep young characters' romantic choices age-appropriate and give them room to say no. Avoid making humiliation the only route to comedy or coercion the payoff for persistence. Audience suitability depends on the actual treatment and distribution context. For a college-facing story, distinguish independence, time, money and responsibility from a generic “more adult” tone.

**Treatment and production context.** The bible’s editorial standard for the 13+ audience keeps minors free from sexualized dialogue, framing and wardrobe direction. Young characters can experience crushes, rejection, envy, affection, grief or fear with their agency intact. Treat coercion as a conflict with consequences. For community participation, offer moderated, age-appropriate routes and a private way to report problems. Separate the evaluator’s observation of content from facts requiring records, such as permission to use a map, music or a performer’s likeness.

**Where the viewer can take part.** A clue the audience can screenshot, a debate they can have offline and an edit they can imagine are useful design tests. A viewer can enjoy the complete story privately. If later development adds a Roblox experience, access depends on its maturity label, account settings, age and region. Roblox reserves Restricted content for eligible age-checked adults. A stated 13+ target also does not settle a video’s YouTube audience classification. For an actual Minecraft production or promotion, check the applicable commercial-use terms and permissions for the footage, music and assets. The present slate uses original Roblox settings. ### Format starting points by audience

The bible’s runtime proposals are prototypes. Keep the same dramatic promise when comparing lengths, and report where the difference changes what the viewer can understand.

| Primary | First-contact treatment to compare | Deliberate viewing alternative | Deciding observation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lore Keepers | A 60–90-second choice between two people with history | A 12–25-minute ensemble episode or shorter linked chapters | Does a private act in an earlier scene make a later public choice more compelling? |

**Representation and accessibility.** In UCLA’s 2026 youth study, respondents raised the issue of credible depictions of neurodivergence. Give a character interests, limits, relationships and an objective that can change. Let a person familiar with that experience review the characterization. For the audience, inspect spoken information in captions (including meaningful sound and speaker changes), visual-only clues that need description or contextual dialogue, small chat overlays, and staging that relies solely on color. A mute pass and a listening pass answer different questions. If the horror clue is a three-note sound, a meaningful caption can signal its recurrence without revealing the mystery. When a clue is only a name on a tiny sign, let the action or accessible description convey what matters. **Audience participation and privacy.** Treat posting, joining a server, buying an avatar item and appearing in a production as optional activities with different motivations. Preserve the consequence and relationship. Changing only names and subtitles can leave the social scene incomprehensible. When an American setting is part of the appeal, keep it American and make the cost visible.

## Development brief

Put the following fields at the top of every show treatment. Keep the brief with the show through edit so the finished work can be reviewed against the original intention. For a single request without a specified audience, start with Lore Keepers. For an existing or explicitly assigned show, honor the declared cohort.

|---|---|
| Primary audience | One exact tier number and name, plus one sentence about the behavior that makes this person the intended viewer. Add one optional secondary audience and the one adjustment it needs. |

| Promise | “This viewer comes for ___, gets ___ in the first chapter and returns for ___.” Use a specific pleasure or relationship, something the viewer can recognize at the end. |

**A filled audience brief, for scale.** Primary **Tier 1 · Lore Keepers**. Secondary **Tier 3 · Scream Team**. A US 17-year-old returning series viewer watches with a friend in messages after rehearsal. The promise is the pleasure of recognizing when two old friends stop trusting each other. ### From the audience card to a show treatment

For a full treatment, give the working title, a logline a nonplayer could repeat, the antagonist or constraint, and what keeps the cast together. ### Concept review before commissioning

Use the supplied bible’s nine 1–5 ratings as a development checklist. ## Finished-show audit

**Question to answer:** Given the declared primary audience, how well does the finished work deliver the experience it promised, where does the fit break, and what evidence would distinguish a creative problem from a distribution problem? This is an editorial assessment. Report observed audience response in its own field if actual viewing data accompanies the show.

### Inputs and inspection

| Material available | What the audit can judge |
|---|---|
| Final episodes, viewing order, intended discovery clip, release language or captions, and original audience brief | Review the show as a returning viewer, then re-enter through the supplied discovery asset as a stranger. Identify the exact version, episodes, runtime, language, materials inspected and any material gaps. |
| Only a script or transcript | Judge wants, causality, dialogue, promises and payoffs visible in the text. Mark performance, framing, sound, caption readability and edit timing **Unassessed**. Cite scene, page or transcript time as available. |
| Only a trailer, clip, synopsis or sample of episodes | Report a clip, premise or sampled-episode verdict. Identify whether the pilot, middle, finale or localization is missing. Do not infer a season payoff from a trailer. |
| No original primary stated | Propose the closest named cohort and explain the evidence and nearest alternative. Mark **inferred target** and the verdict provisional. When the original brief is provided, rerun against it. Preserve a disagreement between the delivered work and its intended audience. |

For a full-season review, inspect every supplied episode before issuing a season verdict. Make one short row per episode with the opening promise, choice, payoff, changed relationship or rule, and outstanding question. Trace at least one promise from the pilot through a middle installment to its payoff or disappearance in the finale. If the work is a standalone short, judge whether its own promise resolves and whether another meeting with this cast is desirable. Record the actual limits of the review; no invented timestamps, quotes or audience reactions.

### Common review criteria

Record **2 · Demonstrated**, **1 · Partial**, or **0 · Missing** for each of eight craft criteria. Record **U · Unassessed** if the material needed to judge a criterion is unavailable. A score of zero means the reviewer could inspect the relevant material and found the required evidence absent. Cite a timestamp and episode, or a scene or page for a script. A missing item should specify the scope searched.

| Universal craft criterion | What to inspect |
|---|---|
| Entry and promise | The released opening and discovery clip make a specific promise. A new viewer can identify who wants what. |
| Character agency | Competing wants lead to a voluntary decision with a consequence. |
| Reward and continuity | The episode resolves something now and carries a change into the next relevant encounter. |
| Game-world staging | The set, framing and world rules make the decisive action intelligible. |
| Age and life stage | The character’s obligations and freedoms make sense for their stated age and setting. |
| Voice | Different people use different registers. Review spoken lines, on-screen text and packaging in the intended language. |
| Access | The intended viewer can distinguish speakers and follow decisive sounds, images and clues on the target device. |
| Suitability and rights | Inspect for age-appropriate treatment, appropriate use of third-party assets and evidence of permission where needed. Missing clearance records are unassessed. A substantiated issue is reported first for resolution. |

Keep the originally declared primary visible when a different cohort scores higher. Never average the five scores into one audience number.

### Verdict and return format

- **Strong editorial fit** requires a primary cohort score of 16–20, no demonstrated dealbreaker and universal craft of at least 10. Flag any outstanding issue in suitability or rights separately.
- **Promising with revisions** means the primary cohort scores 11–15, or scores at least 16 with universal craft below 10. Identify the scene where a revision could restore the promise.
- **Weak fit for the declared cohort** means a primary score of 10 or below, including a demonstrated dealbreaker. Name a stronger adjacent fit if the work supports one.
- **Insufficient material** applies when unknowns could change the requested verdict, or an episode or season judgment exceeds the material inspected. A script can still receive a separate narrative assessment.

Return the show version, declared or inferred primary and original promise. Give the five-cohort matrix, universal craft score and primary verdict. Include an episode log and criterion-level evidence, the strongest counterexample, the revisions in priority order and what the cut should preserve. Record any observed viewer behavior separately with its denominator and follow-up window. End with one audience comparison that could overturn the editorial reading.

### Values and life-stage stakes

In Pew's US survey of 1,391 teen–parent pairs (ages 13–17, September–October 2024), 74% of teens said social media helped them feel connected to friends and 63% saw a place for creative expression. Girls more often than boys described feeling overwhelmed by online drama (45% versus 34%) and finding a place to express creativity (68% versus 58%). Enjoying a fictional argument and wanting relief from a real one can coexist. A story about group conflict should give the audience somewhere emotionally rewarding to land. [Pew, April 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2025/04/22/teens-social-media-and-mental-health/)

| Audience tension to explore | What an observant writer would put on screen | What to ask a viewer afterward |
|---|---|---|
| Belonging and autonomy | Someone finally gets invited, then learns they are expected to leave a friend behind. Give staying and going a real benefit. | At which moment did the invitation stop feeling good? |
| Credit and competence | The group applauds a finished space. The teammate who kept rebuilding the broken entrance stands outside the frame. | Who did you want to speak first? What did you want them to say? |
| Public performance and private care | A teammate makes the joke everyone repeats, then stays after the others leave to help the person it hurt. | Did the act of repair change your opinion? What else would it take? |
| Relief and recognition | Between tense exchanges, two friends share an ordinary task they can do together without speaking. | Was this the moment you'd skip or the reason you'd come back? |
| Fairness and generosity | A rule applies equally on paper. Only one person can afford the time or resources to obey it. | Did you want an exception, a different rule, or a different person in charge? |
| Escape and emotional truth | A fantastic constraint makes trust visible. Two people remember the same room differently, and only one can get back in. | Which felt familiar even though the situation was impossible? |

**Life stage changes the stakes.** Use these as alternative story lenses to test, not fixed developmental traits. At **13–14**, a ride home, a family rule or a friend's invitation can determine access to the whole group. At **15–17**, test unequal freedom, creative authorship, responsibility for a sibling and balancing work with a team. At **18**, school, work and living arrangements can diverge sharply even among childhood friends. At **19–24**, a long commute, a difficult housemate, a night shift or the embarrassment of making new friends can change who gets to show up. Ask whether the *circumstance* fits the person and market. Avoid casting an entire age band as American boarding-school students or residential undergraduates.

## Research and limitations

### Validate the audience prediction

The completed-show audit produces a prediction a researcher can try to disprove. Keep the originally declared cohort fixed for this comparison. Separate what people say they liked, what they choose when alternatives are visible, and whether the same people come back.

1. **Recruit on behavior before taste labels.** Ask for the last game-filmed story watched, how it was found, what happened, whether they chose another part, what they abandoned and what they watched instead. Log play frequency, language of viewing, age and voluntarily described gender separately. Include quiet watchers, nonplayers and game-literate skeptics. Compare college students with same-age nonstudents. Use age-appropriate research recruitment and consent, with private, de-identified reporting.
2. **Compare like with like.** Within each language, randomize concept order and hold presentation quality, protagonist prominence, title clarity and exposure constant. Cross genre with age and gender instead of handing every girl a romance and every boy a horror story. Rotate matched short and long treatments. Include a familiar creator or competing activity as a separate comparison where feasible. See if the advantage follows the genre, the cast, the game surface or the distribution context.
3. **Measure the decision that matters.** Record first choice per exposed viewer, correct unaided explanation of the choice, deliberate selection of the next part per eligible viewer offered it, and voluntary return over the same follow-up window. Track sound-off understanding, title recall and the number who would play instead. Keep click, completion, next-part choice and later return as separate measures. A high completion rate on a very short clip can coexist with low desire for another.
4. **Let contrary evidence change the brief.** If younger or older age cells consistently return more often than the 16–19 core after matching viewing habits and exposure, revisit the creative target. Use separate age cells to test the competing 14–18 range. If girls choose the first clip more but return at the same rate, investigate packaging before claiming a gender preference. If Minecraft-first viewers show stronger return than established Roblox serial viewers across comparable tests, record that route to audience separately and reconsider the recruitment priority. If a localized voice track reverses a ranking, retain a market-specific ranking. Report denominators and uncertainty before promoting any cell.
5. **Keep the source hierarchy intact.** Representative platform or media research can describe its stated population. Creator analytics can describe an actual channel. Public comments and interviews explain possible motives. Choice tests compare particular treatments. A creator's target audience, an avatar's appearance, platform search volume and an AI-generated persona cannot establish the demographic composition of this show's viewers. The outstanding evidence is direct viewer research on these concepts. [Project validation playbook](04-validation-playbook.md)

| Outcome | Denominator and interpretation |
|---|---|
| First choice | People choosing this option divided by those exposed to comparable options. Randomize order. Record when the invitation or a known creator drove the click. |
| Unaided comprehension | Among viewers who saw the relevant scene, ask what changed and for whom. Code the answer before revealing the intended payoff. A surprising but text-supported interpretation can be a creative success. |
| Next chapter | Of viewers offered the same genuine opportunity to choose a follow-up, how many selected it? Include a real alternative and the choice to stop. A hypothetical “would watch” is self-report. |
| Voluntary return | Of eligible earlier viewers with the same opportunity and follow-up window, how many actually returned? State window, sample size, reminders, visibility of the next episode, missing data and overlap. Distinguish individual observations from anonymous aggregate traffic. |
| Cross-cohort or cross-market difference | Compare similar exposure, language, prior creator affinity and play habits. Show numerators and denominators and report uncertainty. Do not use a small audience cell to announce a population ranking. |

### Audit of consequential claims

| Claim or decision | Treatment in this edition | What could change it |
|---|---|---|
| The US comes first and APAC follows | Adopted as market priority. The five profiles can all occur in the US. Country-level repeat viewers for these shows have yet to be measured. | Comparable first-party geography and return data across titles, plus matched US and Philippine concept choices. |
| Ages 16–19, girls and drama | Adopted the age as the creative center and girls who already seek serialized stories as a priority recruitment group. The published US youth evidence supports testing friendship alongside romance. A mobile panel that excludes under-17s cannot establish the gender mix of younger fans. | Voluntarily reported audience demographics paired with actual continuation choices, with recruitment and missing data disclosed. [UCLA’s 2026 US study](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/releases/annual-teens-screens-report-reveals-young-audiences-redefining-modern-storytelling) · [Apptopia’s scope note](https://apptopia.com/en/author/adamblacker/page/2/) |
| A later episode has fewer public views than the premiere | Kept the pattern as a question about discovery and continuity. Later uploads have had different time to circulate and can reach different people. A public ratio cannot say how many of the same viewers returned. YouTube’s audience-retention chart describes behavior *within* a video. | A consistent follow-up window on the same eligible viewers, or a consented choice test when cross-episode tracking is unavailable. [YouTube retention report](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en) |
| Money, fandom, brands and age | Household purchases, spending permission, personal income and a viewer’s own transactions are separate fields. Roblox reported stronger monetization for US adults than for under-18s. A college-age viewer may still have no leisure budget. No cohort-specific income or brand share was available. | Country-specific recent purchase diaries, including gifts, family subscriptions, consideration without purchase, and zero spending. [Roblox Q3 shareholder letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1315098/000162828026068235/ex991-q32026earningsshar.htm) |
| Minecraft viewers, creator rankings, trends and genre scores | Preserved Minecraft as a transfer audience, source-linked series as craft examples, and the horror and drama playbooks as concept hypotheses. Unverified worldwide Minecraft age/gender splits, creator superlatives, fixed slang rules and model-generated greenlight scores carry no evidentiary weight. | Dated local evidence, creator-authorized analytics and blind comparative viewing. Preserve the distinction between a listing, a transcript, a full watch and a viewer response. |

### Blind spots that can change the brief

| Missing perspective or confound | Why the engine could misread it | How to resolve it |
|---|---|---|
| People who watch privately, rarely comment or never buy | Public fandom makes the most expressive viewers unusually visible. | Recruit beyond creator channels. Ask for the last episode watched alone and compare quiet viewers with posters. |
| Followers of a performer, editor, soundtrack or game update | A familiar face or song can explain the first choice even when original characters have little appeal. | Compare familiar and unfamiliar casts. Ask what they would still watch after replacing the creator or music. |
| Adolescents at the edges of the target and young adults outside college | Broad “Gen Z” averages hide employment, school stage, spending authority and who can watch at home. | Compare 13, 14–15, 16–17, 18, 19 and 20–24 with the same stories. Include nonstudents, working students, caregivers and free-only viewers. |
| Disabled, neurodivergent, multilingual and shared-device viewers | A missing caption, unclear speaker, costly connection or unreadable clue can look like disinterest in the genre. | Test sound and caption access and actual device conditions. Ask about barriers voluntarily and use local readers. Fix the barrier before interpreting a creative rejection. |
| Gender, cultural identity and family structure | Girls, boys, queer and nonbinary viewers can share a viewing habit while disagreeing about a particular pairing. A character’s background alone supplies little information about loyalty, voice or genre preference. | Vary protagonists across genres. Recruit on previous viewing, invite optional self-description and ask which depiction feels specific or implausible. Keep potentially identifying small-group results private. |
| Short-only fans and viewers who prefer interactive play | A clip or game can offer a complete pleasure. Short completion or hours played may coexist with low demand for serialized fiction. | Offer another chapter, a different clip, a play or build option, and the option to leave. Evaluate each format on the promise it actually makes. |
| Reach outside an English-speaking metropolitan sample | A Manila convenience sample cannot establish preference across the Philippines or APAC. A US-only campaign can make the US appear to have higher natural affinity. | Label recruitment, place, campaign, age, language and device. Compare matched local cuts and expose geographic unknowns. |
| Dated media, slang and creator listings | Old favorites can be enduring, ironic or unfamiliar. A listing or transcript only supports certain claims. | Keep date, population and inspection scope beside the reference. Refresh trend language before production, platform facts before distribution and market figures with new reporting. |

**Inspection record.** Public studies, creator materials, four episode transcripts and a bounded set of 20 comments underpin this edition. Other works range from publisher descriptions to metadata-only leads. No first-party Bumpp show analytics, representative survey of these five cohorts, US or Philippine youth interviews, or comparative viewing test was supplied. The richest current evidence concerns Tier 1’s story mechanics. No tier has a measured share, income distribution or proven retention rate. Direct viewer research is how those claims can change.
