### The science and the art of shows our audience binges, shares, and comes back for

November 21, 2026 · The single reference for bumpp's script-writing engine and its human writers. Companion to the bumpp Audience Intelligence Bible (full cohort dossiers).

**The house style in one line:** stories that feel like they happened on your own server, hurt like a K-drama, and look like an A24 film. (A24 meets K-drama meets Berry Avenue and Brookhaven.)

### 0.2 The pipeline in one view

1. **Brief:** one primary cohort and its promise (A3, E9).
2. **Mine:** pull mechanisms from proven work (E2, L).
3. **Concept:** cohort recipe + engine + mechanisms + all four house ingredients (E7, E3, H1, H2). Every show is planned vertical-first (K5), with the season clock (K3).
4. **Gate 1 (concept):** Bankability Score 10+ of 12 (P3), formula score, Compelling Test, house-style fusion, transformation test (E1.1, E13.6, G1, E2.7).
5. **Build:** relationship web, Secret Ledger, Information Ledger, character cards, season map (E4, E5, E12, E6).
6. **Script:** beats, voice, suspense, house style, script standard (E6.3, E11, E12, H3 to H7, S).
7. **Voice and craft pass:** every scene labeled with its reactions; hooks, suspense, humor, drama, and banter checked (C6); every line through the AI cadence detector and the rewrite protocol, with a voice sheet for every main character (D6, D7, D8).
8. **Script audit:** the automated detector and the human audit (G9), with the report attached to the cover page.
9. **Gate 2 (script):** all gates in Part G, run twice (G7).
10. **Release and learn:** tag, measure, update the library (E10, V10).

### 0.3 The non-negotiables

1. Steal the mechanism, never the story (E2.7).
2. One primary cohort per show, protected when choices conflict (A3).
3. Every chapter changes something, pays something off, and pulls to the next (E1.2).
4. All four house ingredients, used structurally (H1, G1).
5. Sounds like a person who loves these characters, never like a model (Part D, E11). This is the most important rule in the framework.
6. Safety is structural: romance-led leads 18+, nothing sexualized about minors, no suicide or self-harm as plot (E16, Bible 12).
7. **Built to be impossible to stop watching.** Every show is vertical-first and written on the chapter clock (Part K). Taste decides what we show; the clock decides when.
8. **Bet on proven spines.** At least 8 of 10 launch shows run on a proven arc from P1 and hit its obligatory beats; originality lives in execution (Part P).

# Part A. Audience essentials

## A. Who we write for

The full cohort dossiers (personas, media diets, voice, cringe triggers, dialogue banks) live in the Audience Bible. This part carries what every script decision needs.

### A1 Who we write for: age floor and target (company decision)

| | Ages | What it means for the engine |
|---|---|---|
| **Floor** | 13+ | Nothing is child-directed. No content, packaging, thumbnails, or titles that read as made for children under 13. |
| **Design target (core)** | 16 to 19 | Every show must work first for this viewer. |
| **Ascending** | 20 to 24 | Higher spend; college, work, and adult-life stories. |
| **Spillover** | 13 to 15 | Will watch because teens watch up. Not a design target. A safety constraint. |
| **Characters** | 17 to 21 by default | Senior year, gap year, college, first jobs, moving out. Romance-led shows default to leads who are 18+. |

**Why older** [D][I]

- Roblox moves users from Roblox Select to standard accounts at 16, so a 16+ viewer can follow a show into any companion map or event.
- Users 18+ are Roblox's fastest-growing segment and monetize 50%+ more than under-18s.
- It matches the North Star's 16 to 22 ICP and makes the "A24 meets K-drama" register credible.
- It positions every channel clearly as teen- and young-adult-directed, which supports keeping comments and community features on YouTube. (COPPA "made for kids" turns on under-13 direction; 13+ positioning already avoids it, and 16+ makes it unambiguous. Counsel should confirm.)

**What it costs** [O][I]

- The most intense Roblox-drama fans today are 13 to 15. We trade some raw fandom energy for spend and creative range.
- At 16 to 24 we compete with K-dramas, prestige teen TV, and microdrama apps, so production quality has to carry more weight.

### A2 The five cohorts

Five concentric cohorts, ordered by how likely they are to resonate with drama, horror, and serialized stories filmed inside games. Cohort 1 is the bullseye. Each cohort outward is larger, less committed, and needs a stronger hook.

| Cohort | Name | Ages (core) | Gender skew | Relationship to the format | Est. reach | Primary genres |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Lore Keepers** | 15 to 19 | Strongly female | Already watch Roblox series; audition, edit, theorize | Smallest, most valuable | Academy drama, romance, rivalry, glow-up, mystery |
| 2 | **The Drama Girls** | 16 to 22 | Strongly female | Watch K-dramas, teen TV, BookTok, microdrama clips; play DTI and Royal High; don't yet watch Roblox series | Large | Romance, revenge, fantasy royalty, friendship drama |
| 3 | **The Scream Team** | 15 to 21 | Mixed, slight male | Play Roblox horror, watch lore and theory content, went to Backrooms in a group | Large | Horror, mystery, survival, sci-fi, thriller |
| 4 | **The Lobby** | 15 to 21 | Male-leaning | Play everything; watch gaming YouTube, brainrot, anime; Minecraft SMP lore | Largest in-platform | Comedy, action, competition, lore, anime-style fantasy |
| 5 | **The Crossover** | 18 to 24 | Mixed | College-age players, lapsed players, microdrama-app viewers, international young adults | Large, high spend | College and workplace drama, reality, satire, nostalgia, prestige horror |

Each cohort has a full dossier in Bible Sections 3 to 7, built in the same order so the engine can compare them field by field: cohort card, snapshot, personas, behaviors, media diet, emotional jobs, tropes, characters, voice, cringe triggers, what wins them, format, deepening, dialogue bank, and resonance scorecard. With the 16+ target, Cohorts 2 and 5 move from the edge toward the center of the slate, and Cohort 1 is served through its older members (16 to 19).

### A3 One primary cohort per show, and how to choose between neighbors [P][I]
Every show has exactly one primary cohort: the pleasure its creators promise and will protect when two choices conflict. A secondary cohort can get an adaptation as long as the primary audience's reason to return stays intact.

| Cohort | The viewer's reason to choose another part | The distinction that changes the writing |
|---|---|---|
| Lore Keepers | "I know what that meant between those two." | Accumulated relationship history changes the meaning of a present action. Give the best friend a continuing life. |
| Drama Girls | "I need to see which one will actually choose the other." | Familiar relationship tension can pull them past unfamiliar avatars. Earn a reversal through agency on both sides. |
| Scream Team | "I saw it change. Who else knows?" | An observable rule gives fear, prediction, and release, alone or as a group. |
| The Lobby | "That's our teammate. I want to see how they get out of this." | A legible challenge or joke opens the door; a choice with a social cost makes watching worth time away from play. |
| The Crossover | "My roommate would watch this even if I never mentioned Roblox." | A genre or real-life stake must work for a newcomer on first contact. |

**Choosing between neighbors.** A mystery about a missing teammate is Lore Keepers if a prior friendship makes a witness's omission hurt, Scream Team if working out the rule is the main pleasure, and The Lobby if the group must decide who gives up the winning position. For a relationship premise: if knowing this particular cast is the draw, it's Lore Keepers; if people will cross from another medium for the pairing or trope, it's Drama Girls. If it survives mainly on a recognizable dilemma and a friend's recommendation, it's The Crossover.

**The tiebreaker question:** name the one payoff the show would protect if the runtime were cut in half.

**Viewing-habit tags** (record alongside the cohort, no numbers): returning Roblox-series viewer · Minecraft or other serialized-world viewer · primarily a player · clip-first viewer · genre-first newcomer. "Scream Team · Minecraft-first · watches with a cousin" tells writers more than a demographic label.

### A4 The ten truths about this audience

1. **The core viewer is a teen girl who already follows long-form drama made inside games.** The genre exists, has stars, has studios, and has fandoms. We are entering a scene, not inventing one. Under the 16+ target she may watch more than she plays: girls' gaming drops off after 14 (Bible A1.7). [O][D]
2. **We design for 16 to 19, ascend to 20 to 24, and accept 13 to 15 as spillover.** The existing Roblox-drama fandom skews 13 to 17, but bumpp deliberately aims one step older: it aligns with Roblox's 16+ standard accounts, the North Star's 16 to 22 ICP, and the higher-spending 18+ segment. See Bible Sections 2.1 and A1.4. [D][O][I][NS]
3. **Episode one is the show, and the return is the goal.** Across every studio sampled, episode one has 3x to 7x the public views of later episodes. Public totals are not same-viewer retention (Bible A2.5), but the pattern says the hard problem is getting someone to choose part two. Resonance means a voluntary return (Bible A3.1). [O][P]
4. **The cast is the franchise.** Studios run podcasts, cast Q&As, "Sip or Spill" interviews, cooking videos with the cast, and public auditions. Fans follow the actors (the players behind the avatars) as much as the characters. [O]
5. **Friendship carries as much weight as romance.** In the 2026 UCLA Teens and Screens survey, 57.3% want more platonic relationships on screen and "friendships and social groups" ranked as the #2 thing they want to see. Romance hooks; friendship retains. [D]
6. **Hope beats nihilism.** The #1 request in the same survey: "hopeful, uplifting stories with people beating the odds." Partying, drugs, and drinking ranked last. Dark is fine. Bleak is not. [D]
7. **Horror is a co-equal pillar, not a side genre.** Young YouTube-native horror (Backrooms, Obsession) owned the 2026 box office with audiences mostly under 25, and survival-horror games sit in Roblox's top 10. [D]
8. **Fashion is plot.** Avatars, outfits, and "how to get this look" tutorials are content in their own right. This is where Blueberry's catalog connects to the shows. [O][I]
9. **International is the majority.** APAC alone has about 1.75x the Roblox daily users of the US and Canada (42M vs 24M in Q3 2026), and roughly five in six users are outside US/Canada. Write stories that travel; avoid plots that only work in an American high school. [D]
10. **Cringe is fatal and fast.** Gen Alpha publicly "reset" its meme vocabulary on January 1, 2026. Dated slang, adult-coded "hey besties" energy, and trying-too-hard captions lose this audience on frame one. [D][NS]

### A5 Key numbers to keep in mind

- Roblox had 138 million daily users in Q3 2026; age-checked users split 34% under 13, 37% aged 13 to 17, 29% aged 18 and over. Adults are the fastest-growing group. [D]
- About 30% of US 13 to 17 year olds use Roblox daily; 49% of US teens use it at all. [D]
- Daily users by region: APAC 42M, Rest of World 40M, Europe 32M, US and Canada 24M. US and Canada still produce over half of revenue. [D]
- Girls' gaming drops with age: 81% of US girls aged 13 to 14 play video games vs 67% at 15 to 17, and only 17% of teen girls call themselves gamers. The 16+ girl is often reached through drama habits, not play. [D]
- In the US, female weekly Roblox mobile users passed male users in 2026. [D]
- YouTube and Netflix are tied for teens' daily video time; TikTok is teens' favorite app. [D]
- 35% of Gen Z (13 to 24) watch microdramas; the format's audience is getting younger and more male. [D]
- Young viewers most want "hopeful, uplifting stories with people beating the odds," friendships, and lives like theirs; 57% want more platonic relationships on screen. [D]
- YouTube-made horror (*Backrooms*, *Obsession*) owned the 2026 box office with audiences mostly under 25. [D]
- In the reference Roblox series, Episode 1 draws roughly 3x to 7x the public views of later episodes. Getting a viewer to choose part two is the central problem. [O]

# Part D. Dialogue and voice training

## D. How this audience actually talks, and how to write it so it doesn't sound like AI

This is the most important part of the framework. Structure gets a viewer to Episode 2; voice decides whether she believes a word of it. Every script passes D6 and D7 before any gate is scored. [I]

### D1. Why AI dialogue fails (diagnosed on our own drafts)
Models write dialogue that is clean, balanced, and finished. Real people, especially 16 to 22 year olds, talk in fragments, restarts, deflections, and filler, and almost never say the important thing directly. Our own first drafts of Scripts Vol. 1 failed in these specific ways:

| Failure | Example from our first drafts | Why it reads as AI |
|---|---|---|
| **The narrator in the action lines** | "That lands. We see it land." · "That's it. That's the evidence. Every viewer knows what it means." · "It is not just her room." · "That's true, and everyone hates that it's true." | The script tells the audience what to feel. A real script describes only what the camera sees. |
| **The fragment cadence** | "She almost laughs. Doesn't." · "Ms. Reyes almost smiles. Doesn't." · "Not unkindly." | A rhythm models repeat until it becomes a signature. Once is style; five times is a tell. |
| **Call-and-response banter** | "You're here." / "I live here." / "Again." · "You don't have jokes." / "That's why it's a rule." | Every line answers the last one perfectly and ends on a button. Real conversations miss, overlap, and drift. |
| **Everyone is articulate** | "Because if he takes me back, he'll come. He said he'd go anywhere. Before." | Characters explain their motives in complete, well-built sentences. Real people circle it, say half of it, or say it wrong. |
| **Poignant numbers** | "eleven weeks," "fourteen times," "six years in one look" | Precise numbers used for emotional weight, over and over. |
| **"The way you do when"** | "the way you do when you don't want to cry in front of anyone" | Narrator wisdom about human behavior. Cut it; show the behavior. |
| **No filler, no restarts** | Almost no "like," "um," "wait," "no because," "i mean" in 24,000 words of teen dialogue | Real teen speech is full of them, and they carry meaning (hesitation, softening, stalling). |
| **Same voice for everyone** | Swap Tristan's and Owen's lines and nothing breaks | Each character needs their own vocabulary, rhythm, and way of avoiding things. |
| **Stunted dialogue (the overcorrection)** | Our second draft cut every speech down to fragments: about half of all lines were five words or fewer, the median line was 4 to 7 words, and across ten pilots only one speech ran past 30 words. Scenes became ping-pong of "Okay." "Yeah." "I know." | Withholding everything is as fake as explaining everything. Real people, especially young people, talk a lot: they ramble, tell stories, riff, and go off. The subtext lives in what they talk about instead, not in silence. |

### D2. What great shows do (technique, never their lines)
We study how acclaimed writers make young characters sound real. We take techniques, never dialogue. [D][K]

| Show or film | Technique | How we use it |
|---|---|---|
| ***Eighth Grade*** (Bo Burnham, A24) | The script is full of "um" and "like," much of it taken from real teen YouTube vlogs; the characters talk in long, rambling, more-or-less coherent sentences with perfectly timed filler [D]. Burnham noticed teen girls doing "very complicated things, moment-to-moment" when they speak [D]. | Write the filler on the page. Let a character take a long, wobbly run at a sentence and land it badly. Source speech patterns from real creator videos, not from other scripts. |
| ***Derry Girls*** | Everyone talks at once; friends roast each other constantly; a small problem escalates into chaos through overlapping lines [K] | Group scenes overlap. Write interruptions. Affection is expressed as insult. |
| ***Heartstopper*** | Understatement; the big feeling is said in very few words or in a text; long pauses do the work [K] | The most important line in a scene is the shortest one. |
| ***Euphoria*** | A voiceover narrator who is funny, brutal, and specific about other people's histories [K] | Voiceover sparingly, in a character's own voice, never as the writer's. Adult content does not transfer. |
| ***Sex Education*** / ***Never Have I Ever*** | Characters over-explain when nervous, make impulsive bad decisions, and are wrong out loud [K] | Let nervous characters ramble. Let the lead be wrong and keep talking. |
| ***Reply 1988*** | Neighbors and friends bicker about nothing (food, chores, who sat where) and the love is in the bickering [K] | Fights about tiny things carry the big feelings underneath. |
| ***Weak Hero Class*** | A lead who barely speaks; silence and action do the talking [D][K] | One character per show can be nearly silent. Their few lines matter. |
| ***Love Island USA*** | The gap between what people say in the group and in the confessional; catchphrases that spread because they're specific to a person [D][K] | Give each character one phrase that's theirs, not a generic slang term. |
| ***Walden Academy*** commenters | Fans quote and react to the comic exchanges, not the speeches [O] | The lines that travel are short, specific, and funny, not profound. |

### D3. How they actually talk (spoken)
**Discourse markers (use constantly, vary by character)** [K][I]

- Openers and pivots: *wait* · *okay but* · *no because* · *so like* · *i mean* · *literally* · *ok so* · *bro* (boys and many girls) · *girl* (girls, some boys) · *dude* (older, or ironic)
- Softeners: *kind of* · *lowkey* · *i guess* · *or whatever* · *it's fine* (it's not fine)
- Intensifiers: *so* · *genuinely* · *actually* · *insane* · *crazy* ("that's crazy" is a full response to anything, including tragedy) · *deadass* (character-specific)
- Truth markers: *be so fr* · *fr* · *i'm being so serious* · *no for real*

**Patterns** [D][K][I]

- **Restarts and self-corrections:** "she was, like, no, okay, she didn't say it, but she *said* it."
- **Trailing off:** "and then he just... yeah." The listener fills it in.
- **Under-reacting to big things:** "oh. okay." · "that's crazy." · "cool cool cool."
- **Over-reacting to small things:** "i'm actually going to die" about being out of oat milk.
- **Answering a question with a question,** or with a joke, or with "what?" to buy time.
- **Repetition for emphasis:** "no. no no no." · "stop. stop it."
- **Roasting as love:** close friends insult each other; politeness means distance.
- **Reported speech as performance:** "and she goes, 'oh i didn't see you,' like, ma'am."
- **Specific shared references** instead of explanations: "it's the vending machine thing again."

**Slang density rule** [I]

- Each character has a personal lexicon of **two or three signature words or phrases**, drawn from the Bible's stable list (Bible 8.6). Not the whole dictionary.
- Most characters: no more than one slang term every four or five lines. Volatile slang (Bible 8.6) only for the one character it defines, and expect it to date.
- Characters 20 to 24 use less slang and more irony; therapy vocabulary appears as a joke or deflection.
- Never use slang as a costume or punchline for a racial or cultural group (Bible 8.8).
- **Swearing:** our floor is 13+. Mild only ("hell," "damn," "pissed"), sparingly, and only when it's in character. Most of the time, "freaking," "oh my god," or silence works better.

### D3b. Rhythm: how good dialogue actually moves
Good dialogue alternates. Quick exchanges build speed; then one person goes off for a paragraph; then someone undercuts it with three words. The *Eighth Grade* script is a useful model here: its young characters talk in long, rambling, more-or-less coherent sentences, with the filler in exactly the right places [D]. Terse is a choice for a moment or a character, not a house style. [I]

**The five shapes every episode needs** [K][I]

| Shape | What it is | Example from our scripts |
|---|---|---|
| **The run** | A character goes off for 40 to 120 words: a rant, a story, a nervous ramble, a defense, a confession that keeps going after it should stop | Kayleigh on the jet ski; Trinity on moving to Austin "for a guy who sold vintage lamps"; Owen on waiting at the fountain |
| **The bit** | Two or three friends build one joke over four to eight lines, each topping the last | The *Maple Court* rhyme bit ("Fort." "Sport." "Tort." "What's a tort?") |
| **The story** | Someone retells something with reported speech ("and he goes...") | Kayleigh: "Dev's dad goes, 'who wants to try the jet ski'" |
| **The talk-past** | Two people having different conversations at once, or a speech interrupted and restarted | Brielle's bathroom rant while Gianna keeps trying to stop her |
| **The drop** | After a run, a very short line that lands it | "Nobody wants your aunt." |

**House targets per long episode** (editorial targets, to be calibrated against the youth panel; not industry statistics) [I]

- Short lines (five words or fewer): about **25 to 40%** of all lines. Over 45% reads stunted; under 20% reads speechy.
- Median line: about **7 to 12 words**.
- At least **three runs** of 40+ words per long episode; at least **one per two vertical chapters**.
- At least **one bit** per episode in any show with a friend group.
- No more than **six consecutive one-liners** unless the scene is a deliberate rapid-fire (an argument at full speed, a horror beat, a match call).
- Stock replies ("Okay." "Yeah." "I know.") as complete lines: **no more than three per scene.**
- Exceptions by design: horror tension scenes, match play-by-play, and one deliberately quiet character per show.
- **Vertical mode (Part K):** short lines 30 to 50%; median 5 to 10 words; at least five conflict runs of 25 to 45 words per 10-chapter drop; no speech over about 45 words (15 seconds), and every long speech carries conflict.

**Subtext by volume.** The best way to not say the thing is to say a lot about something else. Kayleigh never says she misses Brielle; she tells a story about Brielle sitting at the back of her grandpa's funeral. Josiah never says he loved Marisol; he lists, at length, everything that happened after she didn't come outside.

### D4. How they text (on-screen texts must be exact)

| Rule | Detail | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Lowercase by default | Capitals read as intense or formal; caps lock is volume | [D] |
| A period means something | "ok" is neutral; "ok." is a fight. Gen Z interviewees read a final period as annoyance, finality, or passive aggression. | [D] |
| 😭 and 💀 are laughter | In one UCLA study, Gen Z used the crying emoji to show laughter in 42% of responses, the opposite of Gen X's 😂 | [D] |
| 🙂 and 👍 are cold | Read as passive-aggressive or dismissive | [D] |
| Letter-stretching | "nooo," "stoppp," "LMAOOO," "PLSSS" | [D] |
| Shorthand | fr · ngl · tbh · rn · idk · gn · bet · icl · istg · wyd | [D] |
| Rhythm | Several short texts in a row, not one long one. The rare paragraph is an event. | [K] |
| "k" | Hostile | [K] |
| Reactions as replies | A heart or a "haha" reaction instead of words, which itself can be a message | [K] |
| Typing and deleting | The typing bubble that appears and disappears is plot | [K] |

### D5. How they talk in Roblox (voice chat and text chat)

- **Voice chat:** "wait wait wait" · "ur mic is so bad" · "you're cutting out" · "bro lagged" · "i'm muting you" · "who's breathing" · "brb my mom" · talking to someone off-mic mid-sentence. [K]
- **Text chat:** short, lowercase, fast, typos left in. "gg." "ez" (taunt). "wait." "omw." "brb." "afk." Older memes ("oof") used ironically. [K]
- **The chat filter:** Roblox replaces filtered words with hashtags (####). A character's message getting filtered at the worst moment is a real, funny, specific detail. [K]
- **Roleplay terms:** OOC ("ooc, brb"), IC, FRP, godmodding (Bible 8.9). [D][K]
- **Mixed modes:** in the same scene, one character speaks on voice while another can only type because their mic is broken. That asymmetry is drama and comedy.

### D6. The AI cadence detector (run on every script)
Search the script for each pattern. Every hit gets rewritten unless it is doing something deliberate and used once.

| # | Pattern | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Narration that interprets ("That lands." "It is not just X." "Everyone knows what it means.") | Delete. Describe only what the camera sees and hears. |
| 2 | "She almost [verb]. Doesn't." and other fragment cadences | Once per script, maximum. Otherwise, show the action plainly. |
| 3 | "The way you do when..." / narrator wisdom | Delete. |
| 4 | Perfect call-and-response (each line answers the last and ends on a button) | Break it: have someone miss the point, overlap, change the subject, or not answer. |
| 5 | Characters explaining their motives in clean, complete sentences | Don't shorten it; make it messier. Let them go sideways, contradict themselves, get distracted by a detail, and arrive at the point by accident. |
| 6 | "It's not X. It's Y." / "Both of those are true." / balanced aphorisms | Rewrite as a plain statement or an action (E11.3). |
| 7 | Poignant precise numbers used for weight | Keep at most one per script. Real people round, guess, and exaggerate. |
| 8 | No filler, no restarts, no trailing off | Add them where the character is nervous, lying, stalling, or close to someone. |
| 9 | Everyone sounds the same | Run the swap test (D7). Rebuild the voice sheets (D8). |
| 10 | Quip button after an emotional moment | End on action, silence, or a line that misses. |
| 11 | Texts with perfect punctuation and capitals | Apply D4. Periods only when someone is mad. |
| 12 | Slang density too high, or slang from the dated list | Apply D3's density rule and Bible 8.6. |
| 13 | Profound lines | Real people at 17 don't speak in quotes. The quotable line should be funny or blunt, not wise. |
| 14 | "Quietly," "softly," "barely" as dialogue tags on every emotional line | Use once or twice an episode; let the line carry it. |
| 15 | A beat or a pause before every important line | Pauses are earned; most important lines come fast and messy. |
| 16 | **Stunted rhythm:** more than 45% of lines at five words or fewer; no speech over 30 words | Add runs, bits, and stories (D3b). Let someone talk. |
| 17 | Ping-pong: more than six consecutive one-line exchanges with no change in rhythm | Break it with a run, a story, a third voice, or an action. |
| 18 | Stock replies ("Okay." "Yeah." "I know.") as whole lines, more than three per scene | Replace with something only this character would say. |
| 19 | Ellipses on every other line ("...Okay." "...Yeah.") | One per scene at most; hesitation should come from the words, not the punctuation. |

### D7. The rewrite protocol (do all eight, in order)

1. **Read it aloud.** Any line you stumble on, or that sounds like it was written, gets rewritten.
2. **The mumble test.** Say the line the way a tired 17-year-old would, mumbling. If it can't be mumbled, it's too written.
3. **The swap test.** Swap two characters' lines. If nothing breaks, their voices are the same.
4. **The interrupt test.** In every argument and group scene, find where someone would cut in. Cut there.
5. **The "would she type that" test.** Every on-screen text: would a real 17-year-old type exactly this, with exactly this capitalization and punctuation?
6. **Cut the narrator.** Every action line describes only what the camera sees and hears.
7. **The slang audit.** Count slang per character; check shelf life (Bible 8.6); check it fits who they are. Then **the rhythm check:** count lines by length and check the D3b targets (share of short lines, median length, runs and bits, longest one-liner streak, stock replies per scene).
8. **The real-person check.** Before production, the youth reader panel (16 to 22, paid) reads every script and marks every line they'd never say. Those lines go.

# Part C. Craft: hooks, suspense, humor, drama, and banter

## C. The four feelings every scene must deliver

Every scene in a bumpp script earns its place by producing at least one of four reactions in the viewer: **a laugh, a gasp, an ache, or a question.** A scene that produces none gets cut or rewritten. The best scenes produce two (a laugh that turns into an ache; a question that turns into a gasp). This part is the craft for each. [I]

| Reaction | What it feels like | Main tools | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Laugh** | Delight, recognition, "this is literally us" | C3 humor, C5 banter | Friend scenes, openings, recovery after fear |
| **Gasp** | Surprise, dread, "wait what" | C1 hooks, C2 suspense | Cold opens, reveals, chapter ends |
| **Ache** | Longing, guilt, loyalty, loss | C4 drama | Quiet scenes, confrontations, the run (D3b) |
| **Question** | "What happens next?" "Who did it?" | C1 hooks, E12 Question Stack | Every ending |

**Scene check:** label every scene with its reactions before scripting. If two scenes in a row produce the same single reaction, change one.

### C1. Sticky hooks: the tried-and-true openings and endings

**Openings that hold the first 3 to 15 seconds** [D][K][I]

| # | Hook | Why it works | Proof it performs | Our example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mid-story entry:** open on someone already talking, laughing, or fighting | Viewers join a conversation instead of being introduced to one | Microdrama practice: pressure first, never an establishing shot [D] | Kayleigh's jet ski story, which dies mid-word when Brielle walks up |
| 2 | **The flash-forward:** show the most dramatic image, then "three days earlier" | Creates a destination the viewer needs to reach | Standard microdrama and high-performing Roblox series structure [D][O] | *Plus One*'s fake couple photo; *Night Shift*'s check-in screen with her name |
| 3 | **The wrong detail:** something familiar with one thing off | Pattern interruption; the viewer leans in to find out why | *Backrooms*' liminal horror; Roblox horror [D] | A torn net tied up with a shoelace; a lamppost someone fixed |
| 4 | **The notification:** a text, a removal, a join message | Native to this audience's real anxiety; reads instantly on a phone | Bible 8.4 [D] | *Kayleigh removed you.* · *isaiah.zz has joined the game.* |
| 5 | **The public humiliation:** a status loss in front of a crowd | Instant empathy plus a promise of payback | Revenge and glow-up titles lead microdrama and Roblox drama [O] | The vote reveal in *B-Side*; the lag freeze in *Unfollowed* |
| 6 | **The impossible line:** a sentence that can't be true | Curiosity spikes | Hidden-identity microdrama hooks [O] | "I didn't send these. I got one too." |
| 7 | **The cute shell, dark core:** something sweet that turns | Contrast; Gen Z responds to childhood turned strange | *The Amazing Digital Circus* [D] | Kids' signs above four beds, and a fifth bed |

**Endings that pull the next episode** [D][K][I]

| # | Ending | Rule | Our example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The decision cut:** cut while the finger is over the button | Decisions beat shocks for return; the viewer argues about what she should do | Brielle's thumb over DELETE CHAT; Ximena's finger over LOG PATIENT |
| 2 | **The arrival:** someone walks in or logs in | The single most reliable cliffhanger in microdrama and K-drama | Hailey's car; *dariusss has joined the game* |
| 3 | **The impossible sound or object** | A detail that shouldn't exist | *New Recording 47* playing from inside an empty house |
| 4 | **The message:** the typing dots, the one-word DM | Instant, phone-native, and leaves the answer open | *xavierrr: dont* |
| 5 | **The almost:** interrupted at the moment of confession or contact | K-drama's most-used device [D] | The diner bell in *Plus One* |
| 6 | **The quiet dread:** no shock, just wrongness | Lingers longer than a scare | Footsteps stopping on the top step |
| 7 | **The new question bigger than the answer** | Answer one, open a larger one | Isaiah is alive (answer); then who's logging in? (bigger) |

**Hook rules**

- The first spoken line of a pilot is mid-thought, never a greeting or an introduction.
- The last line of an episode is under 12 words, or there is no line at all.
- Never end two episodes in a row with the same ending type (E6.5).
- Every chapter of a vertical series marks its hook and cut (S1).
- Test hooks before production: three 15-second cold opens per show (V10.3).

### C2. Suspense at the scene level
E12 covers suspense across a season (the Information Ledger, the Question Stack). These are the tools inside a single scene. [K][I]

| Tool | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **The bomb under the table** | Show the audience the danger before the characters see it, then play a normal scene | The audience sees Kayleigh's screenshot; Brielle doesn't |
| **The ticking clock** | A visible deadline inside the scene | Bryce's "three minutes, count me" against rule two at 2:00 |
| **The slow approach** | Something moves toward a character a little at a time | The hoodie avatar walking down the middle of the street |
| **Rule of three, broken on three** | A pattern repeats twice; the third time changes | The hold music: normal, normal, a half step down |
| **The false safe** | A laugh or relief beat right before the scare | "It's Roblox." / "People die in Roblox constantly!" then the join message |
| **The withheld object** | We see the reaction before what caused it | Jordyn's hand over her mouth while we hear only her breathing |

**Suspense rules:** at least one suspense tool per scene in thrillers and horror, and at least one per episode in every genre. Every scare is preceded by a laugh or a quiet beat (the false safe). Never explain the threat in the same scene it appears.

### C3. Humor: how this audience actually laughs
Humor is the fastest route to "this is literally us," and the main reason friend-group clips get shared. It comes from character and specificity, never from jokes a narrator could tell. [K][I]

**The types that work for 16 to 22 year olds** (extends Bible 8.5)

| Type | Mechanism | Example from our scripts |
|---|---|---|
| **Absurd specificity** | An oddly exact detail makes an ordinary thing funny | "It took Señora Díaz's burrito and gave back a rock." |
| **Escalating bit** | Friends build one joke for four to eight lines, each topping the last | The *Maple Court* rhyme bit ending in "tort" |
| **Deadpan undercut** | A long, passionate speech answered with three flat words | Ten lines about the pan / "Nobody wants your aunt." |
| **Status gap** | A character's dignity versus how they actually look | Derek, "Natural leader," chasing a cat in a sweater |
| **The roast** | Insult as affection; politeness means distance | "It has a garage door, Trin. It goes up." |
| **Self-roast** | A character gets ahead of the joke about herself | Trinity's Austin lamp guy |
| **Meta and online** | Characters know tropes, memes, and how this looks | "This is literally how people die in movies." / "It's Roblox." |
| **Game-native comedy** | The medium's quirks, played straight | The lag freeze; a dramatic exit clipping through a wall; the chat filter eating a key word |
| **The callback** | An earlier joke returns in a new context, often sad | "It's Roblox," said once more at the worst moment |
| **Kid logic** | Younger characters' rules, played seriously | "You're not the priest, you're the cake." |

**Comedy rules**

- **Specific beats general.** "A guy who sold vintage lamps on Instagram" is funny; "a guy" is not.
- **The funny word goes last.** Build the line so the surprise lands at the end.
- **Rule of three:** two normal items, the third one wrong.
- **React, don't explain.** Nobody says "that's funny." Someone just responds.
- **Comedy lives next to pain.** Our best laughs sit one beat before an ache (Kev's "Can we stop looking at me?").
- **Never punch down** (Bible 8.5): no jokes at bodies, race, disability, sexuality, gender identity, or poverty itself.
- **Horror needs laughs.** Scream Team viewers want gallows humor to release tension before the next scare (Bible 5).
- **Minimum:** two clear laughs per long episode in drama and horror; four or more in comedy-forward shows (*Carry*, *Confessional*, *Still Here*); one per two vertical chapters.

### C4. Drama: scenes that hurt in the right way
**The confrontation scene, built to turn** [K][I]

1. **Two wants that can't both win.** Brielle wants the truth; Gianna wants not to pick.
2. **The deflection phase:** both talk around it (humor, logistics, side topics).
3. **The escalation:** one person says something a little too true.
4. **The run:** someone finally goes off (D3b), and it comes out wrong, too much, or sideways.
5. **The line that can't be unsaid:** short, usually under ten words. "I was at the hoop. I was right there."
6. **The exit:** someone leaves, or nobody moves. The scene ends with the relationship changed.

**Stakes that teenagers feel** (E12.6): being left out, last to know, replaced, seen as fake, publicly embarrassed, losing someone without a fight, blamed for something you didn't do, or being the one who did it and hiding it.

**Drama rules**

- **Earn the tears.** No crying in Episode 1 unless the cause is already on screen.
- **Both sides are right about something.** Every confrontation gives the "wrong" person one true point (E13.2).
- **Ache comes from the specific.** A grandpa's funeral, a voice note about pizza rolls, a bracelet made from a charger cord.
- **Let silence follow the run.** After the big speech, nobody fills the gap. Hold it.
- **One great drama scene per episode** is better than three medium ones.

### C5. Banter: the mechanics of real talk between friends
Banter is how the audience falls for a friend group, and it's what gets clipped and shared. [K][I]

| Mechanic | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Yes-and** | Each line accepts the last and adds to it ("Fort." "Sport." "Tort.") |
| **The topper** | Someone tries to end the bit; someone else tops it |
| **The pivot** | Banter swerves into something real without warning, then back out |
| **The running argument** | A small, ongoing disagreement that resurfaces across episodes (the pan; the frog video; Kev and "the incident") |
| **Shared shorthand** | References only the group understands ("the hot tub thing," "the field trip," "the incident") |
| **Overlap and interruption** | Two people start talking at once; someone finishes someone else's sentence wrong |
| **Status play** | Who gets to be the judge, who's always late, who always films |

**Banter rules:** at least one bit per episode in any show with a friend group (D3b); every friend group has two or three running arguments and inside references that pay off later; banter always serves a purpose (it shows the bond before the rupture: Lore Keepers signal 2).

# Part K. The Sticky Engine

## K. Built to be impossible to stop watching

**The verdict that created this part.** Our first ten scripts were built like prestige TV pilots: A24 breathing room in the middle, quiet unscored scenes, long-form episodes, chapters that opened on scene description and sometimes ended on a soft beat. That is not how microdramas, AI-generated or otherwise, hold people for an hour at a time. On paid platforms, nine in ten paying viewers of a flagship AI series were still watching after 100 minutes [D]. Our scripts were not designed to do that. This part fixes it, and it overrides any earlier rule that conflicts with it, including the "slow middle" in H7. [I]

**The principle:** taste lives in *what* we show (specific, true, beautiful, funny). Stickiness lives in *when* we show it. We keep the A24, K-drama, Berry Avenue and Brookhaven taste, and we put it inside the microdrama clock.

### K1. What the format's practitioners report (and how much to trust it)

| Finding | Source and strength |
|---|---|
| Every episode: a 3-second cold open (a face reacting, a shock line, or a reveal in motion), one reversal, and a cliffhanger as the final shot | Practitioner guides [D] |
| Writers now write to the second: ReelShort reportedly found retention spikes when the freeze-frame lands between seconds 55 and 58 of a 60-second episode | Practitioner report via Filmustage [D, single source] |
| The 3-second freeze test: if a stranger needs backstory to understand the frame, it isn't a hook yet | Practitioner guides [D] |
| Friction must be filmable: actual verbal or physical conflict in frame, not implied subtext | Practitioner guides [D] |
| DramaBox reportedly stops recommending an episode to new users if fewer than 70% watch past 0:15; a "door flies open, 'You lied to me'" opening reportedly held 91% vs 43% | Practitioner claim [D, unverified vendor blog; treat as directional] |
| Episodes with three emotional spikes per minute reportedly had 73% higher completion than those with one climax at the end; a mini-cliffhanger every 20 to 30 seconds | Practitioner claim [D, unverified; directional] |
| The payoff must start inside the same clip; pure setup episodes collapse | Practitioner guides [D] |
| Cliffhangers resolve in the first 10 seconds of the next episode, then plant a new hook in the final 5 | Practitioner guides [D] |
| Change the visual every 2 to 3 seconds (angle, zoom, text overlay, reaction shot) | Practitioner guides [D] |
| Melolo's free, ad-supported model rewards density: shorter series (15 to 30 episodes), each packed | Practitioner report [D] |
| A three-part loop, "hook, twist, cliffhanger," in under 90 seconds; no slow build-ups | Practitioner guides [D] |

The vendor claims are not audited, but they agree with each other and with the platforms' own behavior. We treat them as the floor, then test with our own data (K7).

### K2. The chapter clock (every vertical chapter, 60 to 90 seconds)

| Time | Beat | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **0:00 to 0:03** | **HOOK** | A face reacting, a shock line, a notification, or a reveal in motion. Never scene description first. Passes the 3-second freeze test. |
| **0:00 to 0:08** (Ch 2+) | **RECAP-RESOLVE** | Pay off the last chapter's cliffhanger in the first 8 seconds, in a way that raises the stakes. |
| **0:03 to 0:20** | **FRICTION** | Two people, filmable conflict, a clear want. The viewer knows what's at stake by 0:15. |
| **~0:20 to 0:30** | **SPIKE 1** | A laugh, a gasp, an insult, a reveal, a slap of truth. |
| **~0:40 to 0:55** | **SPIKE 2 / TURN** | The one reversal. Something the viewer didn't know changes the scene. |
| **~0:55 to 0:75** | **SPIKE 3** (90-second chapters) | Escalation after the turn. |
| **Last 3 to 5 seconds** | **BUTTON** | Freeze on a face, a decision, an arrival, a message, or an impossible detail. One beat before resolution. Last line under 8 words, or none. |

**Density rules**

- **No more than 10 to 15 seconds without new information, a laugh, or a spike.**
- **At least 2 spikes per 60 seconds, 3 per 90 seconds.**
- **Every chapter pays something off** (an answer, a laugh, a win, a reveal) inside the clip.
- **No speech over about 15 seconds (roughly 40 to 45 words) in vertical**, and every long speech must carry conflict (an accusation, a confession, a rant at someone). Rambling for texture belongs in the long-form compilation, not the chapter.
- **No quiet unscored scene longer than 5 seconds in vertical.** The A24 breath becomes a held 2 to 3 second shot inside a scene, not a scene.
- **Visual change every 2 to 3 seconds** in the edit (angle, reaction, text overlay, UI pop).
- **Every chapter ends on a different button type** than the one before (C1 endings).

### K6. The Stickiness Test (G10)
Score every chapter 0 to 2 on each item. Maximum 12 per chapter. **A chapter passes at 10+; a launch drop passes only if every chapter passes and the average is 11+.**

| Item | 2 = |
|---|---|
| **Hook (0:03)** | Passes the 3-second freeze test: a stranger understands the conflict from one frame |
| **Recap-resolve (0:08)** | The last button is paid off fast and the stakes go up (Ch 1: the premise is clear by 0:15) |
| **Friction** | Filmable conflict between people on screen by 0:15 |
| **Spikes** | 2+ per 60 seconds, 3+ per 90; nothing flat for more than 15 seconds |
| **Payoff in clip** | Something is answered, won, revealed, or laughed at inside this chapter |
| **Button** | Freeze one beat before resolution; last line under 8 words or none; type differs from the previous chapter |

### K6b. The stickiness checker (reference implementation)
The engine runs this on every vertical script. It checks that the beats are present and on time; it cannot judge whether a beat is good. Every chapter uses bold timestamp tags (S1).

```python
"""bumpp Sticky Engine check (K6): per-chapter clock, spike density, gaps, recap and button rules."""
import re, sys
TS = re.compile(r'\*\*\[(\d+):(\d{2})(?: ([A-Z/ \-]+))?\]\*\*')
def chapters(text):
    parts = re.split(r'\n(?=\*\*CHAPTER \d+)', text)
    return [p for p in parts if p.startswith('**CHAPTER')]
def check(ch):
    head = ch.split('\n',1)[0]
    n = int(re.search(r'CHAPTER (\d+)', head).group(1))
    stamps = [(int(m.group(1))*60+int(m.group(2)), (m.group(3) or '').strip()) for m in TS.finditer(ch)]
    secs = [s for s,_ in stamps]; labels = [l for _,l in stamps]
    runtime = (max(secs) + 5) if secs else 0
    gaps = [b-a for a,b in zip(secs, secs[1:])]
    spikes = sum(1 for l in labels if 'SPIKE' in l or 'TURN' in l)
    per_min = spikes / (runtime/60) if runtime else 0
    first = stamps[0] if stamps else (None,'')
    hook_ok = first[0] == 0 and ('HOOK' in first[1] or 'RECAP' in first[1])
    hook_by3 = any(s <= 10 and 'HOOK' in l for s,l in stamps) or (n == 1 and hook_ok)
    recap_ok = n == 1 or any(s <= 8 and 'RECAP' in l for s,l in stamps)
    friction = any(s <= 25 and 'FRICTION' in l for s,l in stamps)
    button = [i for i,(s,l) in enumerate(stamps) if 'BUTTON' in l]
    tail = ch[ch.rfind('BUTTON'):] if button else ''
    spoken = re.findall(r'\n([^\n*\[][^\n]{0,200})\n\nFREEZE', tail) or re.findall(r'\n([^\n*\[][^\n]{0,200})\n+FREEZE', tail)
    last_line_words = len(spoken[-1].split()) if spoken else 0
    score = {
        'hook': 2 if hook_ok and hook_by3 else (1 if hook_by3 else 0),
        'recap': 2 if recap_ok else 0,
        'friction': 2 if friction else 0,
        'spikes': 2 if per_min >= 2 and (not gaps or max(gaps) <= 20) else (1 if per_min >= 1.5 else 0),
        'button': 2 if button and stamps[button[-1]][0] >= runtime - 15 and last_line_words <= 8 else (1 if button else 0),
    }
    return dict(ch=n, runtime=runtime, spikes=spikes, spikes_per_min=round(per_min,1), max_gap=max(gaps) if gaps else 0,
                last_line_words=last_line_words, auto_score=sum(score.values()), parts=score)
if __name__ == '__main__':
    for c in chapters(open(sys.argv[1]).read()): print(check(c))
```

### K7. Measure and tune (our own data decides)

- Track per chapter: 3-second hold, 15-second hold, completion, next-chapter tap, and the second-by-second drop curve.
- Any chapter with a visible drop before 0:15 gets a new hook. Any chapter whose next-chapter tap is below the series average gets a new button.
- A/B test buttons: post two versions of the same chapter's last 5 seconds.
- Write new chapters to the moments where our own retention spikes, not to the guides.

# Part P. Proven arcs: the low-hanging fruit

## P. Bet on structures audiences have already chosen

**The rule.** Originality lives in execution (the world, the characters, the specific details, the voice), not in the spine. Every show is built on an arc spine that audiences have already rewarded at scale, and hits the beats fans of that arc expect. A clever premise with no proven spine is a bet; a proven spine executed with taste is an investment. [I]

**Slate rule (supersedes the cohort mix in E-section slate guidance where they conflict):** at least **8 of 10** launch shows must run on a proven arc spine from P1 with a documented demand signal. At most 2 can be originals without a proven spine, and they must be the cheapest to produce.

### P1. The proven arc library
Each spine lists the evidence that audiences want it, the **obligatory beats** (the moments fans of that arc expect and will feel cheated without), where they land in a 36 to 40 chapter vertical season, and the twist that makes ours fresh.

**1. Enemies to lovers across a class divide**

- **Proof:** the most-cited romance trope on BookTok and Wattpad [D]; *Walden Academy*'s scholarship-student premise drew 1.54M views on Episode 1 [O]; elite-school class romance is a K-drama and streaming staple (*Boys Over Flowers*, *Heirs*, *Maxton Hall*) [K].
- **Obligatory beats:** public humiliation of the outsider (Ch 1) · forced proximity by an institution (Ch 2 to 4) · the first time he sees her competence (Ch 5 to 7) · the care scene: injury, illness, or a bad night (Ch 9 to 12) · jealousy over a second lead (Ch 12 to 16) · he defends her in public, at a cost (Ch 15 to 18) · the almost, then the kiss (midpoint, Ch 18 to 20) · his family threatens her (Ch 22 to 26) · the misunderstanding or sacrifice that splits them (Ch 28 to 32) · the grand public gesture (Ch 36 to 38).
- **Fresh twist:** give him a reason to want to fail, so their goals collide for real.

**2. The fake relationship**

- **Proof:** a top contemporary romance trope [D]; contract romance is a core microdrama engine [O].
- **Obligatory beats:** the deal and its rules (Ch 1 to 3) · the first public performance (Ch 3 to 5) · a rule broken by accident (Ch 6 to 9) · "it's fake, right?" (Ch 10 to 14) · jealousy from outside (Ch 12 to 16) · the real kiss (midpoint) · someone finds out it was fake (Ch 24 to 28) · the end date arrives (Ch 30 to 34) · choosing it for real, publicly (finale).
- **Fresh twist:** the deal betrays a friendship the audience also loves.

**3. Hidden identity and the glow-up reveal (Cinderella)**

- **Proof:** the #1 engine in microdrama rankings: hidden heirs, disguised CEOs, heiress comebacks [O]; stinkella's glow-up revenge title drew 5.56M views [O].
- **Obligatory beats:** mocked for her appearance or status (Ch 1) · the audience learns the secret immediately (Ch 1 to 2) · near-discoveries (every 5 chapters) · a cruel antagonist takes credit for what's hers (Ch 4 to 10) · partial reveals that humiliate the antagonist (every 5 to 6 chapters) · one person learns the truth and keeps it (midpoint) · **the transformation scene**, public, in full (Ch 28 to 34) · the antagonist's exposure.
- **Fresh twist:** the glow-up is on her terms; what she transforms into is herself.
- **Non-negotiable:** deliver the transformation. Subverting it entirely denies the payoff the arc exists for.

**4. The switched-at-birth true heiress**

- **Proof:** *True Heiress vs. Fake Queen Bee* passed 395M views on ReelShort [D]; switched-identity family drama is a K-drama and telenovela staple [K].
- **Obligatory beats:** the reveal in public (Ch 1) · the real daughter arrives in the rich house and is treated as a guest (Ch 2 to 5) · the "fake" daughter's sabotage (Ch 4 to 12) · a parent chooses the fake daughter (Ch 6 to 10) · the real daughter proves herself (every 5 chapters) · the original family's secret (midpoint) · the two daughters see each other clearly (Ch 24 to 30) · a choice between families (finale).
- **Fresh twist:** the two girls become the only people who understand each other, and turn on the adults who lied.

**5. Rejected, then rising (fated-mate fantasy)**

- **Proof:** werewolf and fated-mate romance is one of ReelShort's foundational genres (*Fated to My Forbidden Alpha* was an early breakout) [D]; "rejected mate" is a large dedicated shelf on Goodreads (1,281 books; one series lead title has about 30,000 ratings) and a staple of serial-fiction apps [D]; fantasy romance and emotional revenge dominate 2026 weekly microdrama charts [O].
- **Obligatory beats:** the public rejection (Ch 1) · exile or humiliation (Ch 2 to 4) · found family among outsiders (Ch 4 to 8) · discovery of hidden power (Ch 6 to 10) · the time jump and return, transformed (Ch 10 to 14) · his regret (from the return onward) · a second lead who respects her (Ch 12 to 20) · the truth behind the rejection (midpoint) · she saves the pack that cast her out, on her terms (Ch 30 to 36) · she chooses (finale).
- **Fresh twist:** she doesn't win him back; she wins herself back, and he has to earn a second look.
- **Safety:** leads 18+; no possession, coercion, "claiming," or abuse framed as love (Bible 12).

**6. Second chance with a shared past (dual timeline)**

- **Proof:** a top contemporary trope [D]; the K-drama "met as children" device and dual-timeline ensembles [K].
- **Obligatory beats:** the reunion (Ch 1) · the old ritual shown (Ch 2) · the night it broke, in pieces (every 4 to 6 chapters) · the old wound reopened (midpoint) · the full truth of that night (Ch 28 to 32) · the old ritual, restored (finale).

**7. The wrongly accused outsider and the anonymous account (whodunit)**

- **Proof:** *Gossip Girl* and *Pretty Little Liars* lineage [K]; Walden fans compare their show to *Gossip Girl* and post prediction threads [O].
- **Obligatory beats:** the public accusation (Ch 1) · one unlikely ally (Ch 1 to 3) · a fair clue every chapter · a red herring with real evidence (Ch 8 to 14) · the accused proven partly guilty (midpoint) · the unmasking with all clues paying off (Ch 30 to 34).

**8. Rules horror ("if you work the night shift, follow these rules")**

- **Proof:** rule-list horror is a viral internet fiction format [K]; rule-based survival games sit in Roblox's top 10 [D]; *Backrooms* opened at $81.5M [D].
- **Obligatory beats:** the rules shown in full (Ch 1) · rule broken by someone else (Ch 3 to 6) · a rule that contradicts another (Ch 1 to 8) · the rules' author revealed (midpoint) · the rule that was a lie (Ch 28 to 32).

**9. Found-footage mystery**

- **Proof:** *Backrooms* web series to an A24 record opening [D]; analog horror [K].
- **Obligatory beats:** the impossible recording (Ch 1) · the group investigates by footage · the clue in an old recording · the misdirect breaks (Ch 8 to 10) · the truth on tape.

**10. The elimination game**

- **Proof:** *Squid Game*-themed games on Roblox have hundreds of millions of visits (one passed 459M) [D]; *Squid Game* and *Alice in Borderland* lead the survival-game genre on Netflix [K]; elimination-format reality like *Love Island* drives daily viewing [D].
- **Obligatory beats:** the invitation and the prize (Ch 1) · the first elimination proves the stakes are real (Ch 1 to 2) · alliances (Ch 3 to 6) · a betrayal by an ally (Ch 8 to 10) · a game that forces a friend to vote out a friend (Ch 10 to 15) · who runs the game (midpoint) · the final two (finale).
- **Our stakes:** account deletion, not death. Losing your account is losing your history, items, and friends: real to this audience, safe for a 13+ floor.
- **IP distance:** no tracksuits, masks, dolls, numbered uniforms, or recreated games from any specific show (E2.7).

**11. The idol survival audition** (benched for launch): proven (*Alien Stage*, *KPop Demon Hunters*) [D] but entirely dependent on an original song testing well.

**12. The underdog team** (benched for launch): proven in anime (*Haikyu!!*) [D] but the Lobby audience would rather play than watch, and our drama audience over-indexes elsewhere.

### H1.1 How to use Brookhaven with a 16 to 22 audience
Brookhaven is the most-played world on Roblox, and it skews young. The Bible shows older teen girls play less than younger ones (Bible A1.7). Shown as a trendy present-day hangout, it risks a "that's a kids' game" reaction from our core. So Brookhaven appears in three registers only: [D][I]

- **Memory:** the town you grew up playing in, the street you left, summer 2020 when everyone was online.
- **Home:** where the scholarship kid takes the bus back to; where the designer's work began; the neighbors who knew you before.
- **The uncanny:** the familiar map made strange: empty at 3am, abandoned, changed by someone who shouldn't be there.

Comedy in Brookhaven is welcome when it's chaos the characters are in (a town-wide chase, a reality-show challenge), never when the show is laughing at the game's younger players.

## H3. Taste rules

These separate bumpp from both clickbait Roblox drama and cheap microdrama. Every script and cut gets checked against them.

1. **Specific beats impressive.** A cracked phone case, a group chat renamed eleven times, the LED strip stuck on purple. One true detail outweighs a mansion. (E11.2)
2. **Let one scene per episode be quiet.** Two characters, no music, a mundane task, something unsaid. This is where the audience falls in love. (K-drama domestic moments; A24 stillness)
3. **The gesture is the dialogue.** Saving a seat, remembering an order, fixing a griefed build exactly how she liked it. Feelings are shown in actions far more than said. (E11.5)
4. **Beauty with a crack in it.** Berry Avenue glamour, always with one thing slightly wrong: the free starter shoes under the designer coat, the perfect room with an unmade bed.
5. **Chaos with stakes.** Brookhaven comedy is welcome; every chaotic scene still changes something between two people.
6. **Trust the audience.** No narrator explaining what we just saw. No captions that tell us how to feel. This audience reads subtext better than adults do. (Bible 2.4)
7. **Earn the big emotion.** No crying in Episode 1. The first tears come after we know what they cost.
8. **Fast in, slow middle, hard out.** Open on the hook (microdrama), let the middle breathe (A24), cut out sharply on a decision or an image (K-drama episode endings). **In vertical chapters, Part K overrides this:** the "breath" is a 2 to 3 second held shot inside a scene, never a scene.
9. **Every show has a sound.** An original theme that becomes the show's signature, the way K-drama OSTs mark the big moments.
10. **Endings that trust you.** Pay off the season question; leave one feeling unresolved, the way A24 endings stay with you on the drive home.
11. **No wink.** Characters can be funny and self-aware; the show never mocks its own story or its audience.
12. **Never cheap.** If a shot, a thumbnail, or a line could appear in a ReelShort ad, redo it. (Bible 4.9; North Star)

**The taste test:** pause any frame of the finished show. Would a 17-year-old screenshot it for her Pinterest? Read any line aloud. Would she send it to her group chat? Watch the last 10 seconds. Does she need the next chapter? All three, every episode.

## H4. Visual language inside Roblox

### H4.1 Light and color

| Element | House style | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Time of day | Golden hour, blue hour, late night; the North Star palette of midnight rooms, rain on windows, dim LEDs, lavender fog, moonlight on fabric [Bible 2.6] | Flat noon light on every scene |
| Lighting | Future-lighting engine settings, practical sources (lamps, screens, streetlights), deep shadows, one warm source in cold scenes [I] | Fullbright, no shadows |
| Palette per show | Each show owns a two-color signature (e.g., *Unfollowed*: cold cafeteria white and bruised lavender; *Night Shift*: hospital green and sodium orange) | Saturated rainbow sets |
| Weather and atmosphere | Fog, rain, dust in light, condensation; Roblox atmosphere settings as mood tools | Clear sky by default |
| Faces | Dynamic heads for emotional scenes; facial animation is a production standard this audience notices [O] | Static default faces in close-up |

### H4.2 Camera

| Shot | Use | Source of the instinct |
|---|---|---|
| **The held wide** | Characters small in a big space; loneliness, distance, scale. Hold 3 to 6 seconds longer than comfortable. | A24 |
| **The reaction close-up** | The face that hears the news, held before and after the line | K-drama |
| **The over-the-shoulder phone** | Reading a text with her; we see typing bubbles and deletions | Gen Z native |
| **The static locked frame** | Comedy and dread both work better when the camera doesn't move and the avatar does | A24, Brookhaven comedy |
| **The slow push** | Toward a face at the moment of realization | K-drama, A24 |
| **The POV avatar walk** | First-person through a familiar Brookhaven street; the audience walks it too | Roblox native |
| **The fit reveal** | Slow vertical pan from shoes to face for transformation looks | Berry Avenue culture; E6.7 |
| **The empty frame** | The room after someone leaves it, held | A24 |

Rule: vertical chapters frame for 9:16 first; long-form compilations reframe to 16:9 with intentional composition, not stretched crops.

**Production reality:** A24 relies on actors' faces in long takes; Roblox avatars have limited facial range. In this medium the A24 feeling comes from framing, light, blocking, sound, and silence. Use close-ups only where dynamic heads and animator time are budgeted, and let the body, the room, and the pause do the rest. [I]

### H4.3 Sets

- **Berry-Avenue-grade interiors** for wealth, school, fashion, and bedrooms: lived-in, never showroom-perfect.
- **Brookhaven-style town** for neighborhoods, family, community, chaos, nostalgia: the cul-de-sac, the hospital, the school, the gas station at 3am.
- **One "liminal" location per show:** a space that is empty when it shouldn't be (the mall after close, the pool at night, the school hallway on a Saturday).
- Every recurring set gets a detail that changes over the season (a plant dying, a poster coming down, a light that stays on).

### H4.4 Wardrobe

- Every lead has a signature look and one transformation look (E13.4, Bible 9.4), designed as potential catalog drops.
- Wardrobe tells the story: who borrowed whose hoodie, who stopped wearing the matching bracelet, who changed her look the week after the breakup.
- Mix price points on purpose: designer pieces with free starter items, the way real players dress.
- **Characters under 18:** no body-focused framing, no slow "fit reveal" pans, no sexualized styling (Bible 12.1). Fit reveals are for characters 18+.

## H6. Performance and voice direction

- **Under-play.** The camera is close; the audience can hear a lie in a pause.
- **Let lines overlap** in friend-group scenes; real teens interrupt.
- **The flat delivery of big news** is more devastating than shouting.
- **Laugh-crying is real.** Characters laugh at the wrong moment, then don't.
- **Voice casting:** unknown voices over influencer voices; 40% of young people would rather see a new actor than an influencer (16.8%). [D]

## H7. Editing rhythm: reconciling A24 with microdrama speed

The tension between "let it breathe" and "hook every 60 seconds" is solved by placement, not compromise. [I]

| Part of a chapter or episode | Rhythm | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **First 3 to 15 seconds** | Microdrama speed: cold open on the most arresting image or line | Earns the stay |
| **Middle** | A24 breath: hold the look, let the silence sit, cut on emotion rather than on line | Earns the attachment |
| **Last 5 seconds** | K-drama cut: freeze on a face, a decision, or an image; the theme can sting in | Earns the next chapter |

In vertical chapters, Part K overrides this table: the breath is a held 2 to 3 second shot inside a spike, and nothing goes more than 20 seconds without a beat (K2). The slow middle belongs to the long-form compilation.

### E0.3 The six rules the system never breaks

1. **Steal the engine, not the story.** We take *mechanisms* (structures, dynamics, devices) from what works. We never take characters, plots, names, dialogue, songs, or distinctive expression (Section E2.7).
2. **Every show has one cohort promise** and protects it when choices conflict.
3. **Every episode must change something and pull the viewer to the next one.** The central problem in this genre is getting someone to choose part two: in the reference Roblox series, Episode 1 draws roughly 3x to 7x the public views of later episodes. [O]
4. **Friendship carries as much weight as romance.** 57.3% of young people surveyed want more platonic relationships on screen, and "friendships and social groups" is the #2 thing they want to see. [D]
5. **Sound like a person who loves these characters, never like a model.** Specific, uneven, petty, funny; feelings shown more than stated (Section E11). A structurally perfect script that sounds generic has failed.
6. **Safety is structural, not a filter at the end.** Romance-led shows default to leads aged 18+, minor characters are never sexualized, and nothing from adult source material (adult-rated TV, explicit fanfiction) carries over except abstract structure (Section E16, Bible Section 12).

## E1. The core formula

### E1.1 The series formula
> **RESONANCE = PROMISE × ENGINE × WEB × WORLD × CADENCE × VOICE**

It is multiplicative on purpose: if any factor is zero, the show fails, no matter how strong the others are.

| Factor | Definition | The question it answers | Where to build it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PROMISE** | The specific pleasure this show gives its primary cohort, every episode | "Why would *this* viewer choose another part?" | Section E7 (cohort recipes); Bible cohort cards |
| **ENGINE** | The repeatable conflict generator that produces new episodes without new premises | "What keeps happening after Episode 30?" | Section E3 (series engines) |
| **WEB** | The relationship network: ships, rivalries, friendships, loyalties, secrets | "Who do they care about, and what is unresolved between them?" | Section E4 (relationship web) |
| **WORLD** | The setting plus its rules, status system, and game-native logic | "What can only happen here?" | Section E8 (game translation layer) |
| **CADENCE** | The rhythm of hooks, turns, reveals, and cliffhangers across episode and season | "When does the viewer get rewarded, and what pulls them forward?" | Section E6 (structure) |
| **VOICE** | Dialogue, texting, humor, and register that sound like real 16 to 22 year olds | "Would a 17-year-old cringe?" | Bible Section 8 |

**Scoring the formula (for concepts, before scripting):** rate each factor 0 to 3. Any 0 kills the concept. A total under 12 of 18 goes back for development. A total of 15+ moves to scripting. [I]

### E1.2 The episode formula: HOOK, PRESSURE, TURN, PROOF, PULL
Every episode, at every length, contains these five beats in this order. [D][I]

| Beat | What happens | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **HOOK** | The most arresting image, line, or situation of the episode lands immediately: a face mid-reaction, a shock line, or a reveal already in motion | Never open on an establishing shot. Pressure arrives first; character emerges from the response. |
| **PRESSURE** | Someone wants something and something blocks them. Stakes become clear. | The cohort's promise must be visible here (a relationship strained, a rule tightening, a team at risk) |
| **TURN** | One reversal: new information, a betrayal, a choice, a misunderstanding | Exactly one main turn in a short chapter; two or three in a long episode |
| **PROOF** | The episode delivers a real payoff: an answer, a confession, a win, a scare, a consequence | Answer at least one question every episode. Constant withheld payoffs are a reason to leave. |
| **PULL** | End on a new question, ideally a **decision** rather than only a shock | The last frame is one beat before resolution |

Microdrama practice compresses this to a cold open in the first 3 seconds, escalation with exactly one reversal, and a cliffhanger in the final seconds; writers on those platforms describe every script decision as "what's the hook, what's the reversal, what's the hang." [D] We add PROOF because our audience punishes stories that never pay off (Bible Section 3.9 and 4.9 cringe triggers).

### E1.3 The season formula: the four movements

| Movement | Share of season | Purpose | Must contain |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collision** | First 15% to 20% | Put the web in motion | The inciting status shock, the named ship or rivalry, the friend group, the world rule, the season question |
| **Entanglement** | 20% to 50% | Raise attachment and complications | Forced proximity, the "almost" moments, secrets accumulate, the first pinch point |
| **The Flip (midpoint)** | Around 50% | Change what the story is about | The first kiss or alliance, a major identity reveal, or the rule breaking. K-drama practice places the first kiss and a revealed past connection around Episode 8 of 16, shifting the story from courtship to external conflict. [D] |
| **Reckoning** | 50% to 90% | Consequences arrive | External pressure, the second lead's best case, the late-season rupture (K-drama's familiar Episode 14 to 15 breakup or misunderstanding) [D], the friend group's crisis |
| **Release** | Final 10% | Pay off the season question; open the next | The earned payoff, a cost that sticks, a new question for Season 2 |

**Microdrama adaptation:** if a season is released as 30 to 100 vertical chapters, the most important turn must land before the point where casual viewers decide whether to continue. On paywalled microdrama apps that decision point sits around Episode 10, and "the biggest turns often arrive around" there. [D] We are free, but the attention economics are the same: plant the first major reversal by Chapter 8 to 10 of a vertical season. [I]

### E1.4 The two hard questions every concept must answer before writing

1. **"What keeps happening after Episode 30?"** Microdrama writers describe this as the real question, ahead of "what happens in Episode 1." [D] If the answer is "we'll think of something," there is no engine (Section E3).
2. **"What is the one payoff this show protects if the runtime is cut in half?"** (Bible Section 2.3.) That payoff is the promise.

## E2. The source-mining system: learn from what already works

### E2.1 The principle
Our audience has already voted, in views, edits, fanfiction tags, rereads, and box office, for the stories that hook them. The engine's job is to read those votes, extract the **mechanisms** underneath, and rebuild them natively inside Roblox with original characters and worlds. Ideas, tropes, structures, and dynamics are free to use; specific expression (characters, plots, dialogue, music, art, distinctive sequences) is not. [K]

### E2.5 How the engine mines a new source (the extraction procedure)
When the engine studies any new show, book, webtoon, game, video series, or fandom:

1. **Confirm it performs for our cohorts.** Look for at least two signals: views or charts, fan edits and theories, fanfiction volume, survey favorites, box office, comments that quote lines. Record each with its date and population.
2. **Find the moment fans replay.** The scene that gets edited, screenshotted, quoted, or written about is where the mechanism lives.
3. **Name the mechanism in abstract terms.** "A guarded person chooses sides in public in front of the person whose opinion matters," not "the scene where [character] says [line]."
4. **Explain the pull.** Which cohort need does it meet (Bible cohort cards: what they want to feel)?
5. **Translate into game logic** (Section E8): what is the in-game equivalent of the setting, the status system, the obstacle?
6. **Strip identifying elements:** names, plot sequence, dialogue, distinctive designs, songs, settings unique to the source.
7. **Write a Mechanism Card** (E2.3) and add it to the library with a new ID.

### E2.6 Fandom signal mining: write what fans write
Fans tell us what canon didn't give them enough of. The engine treats these as demand signals:

| Signal | Where it shows up | What it tells the writer |
|---|---|---|
| Fanfiction tags with high volume | AO3 additional tags on popular pairings (fluff, angst, hurt/comfort, slow burn, AU) [D] | Build those emotional experiences into canon: the comfort scene after the hurt, the quiet domestic moment, the AU special |
| Edits | TikTok and YouTube fan edits of a show [O] | Which looks, lines, and pairings carry emotion out of context; design more of those frames |
| Ship names | Fans coined "Romaire" for *Walden Academy*'s leads [O] | Fans want a pairing they can name; give leads names that combine well |
| Prediction and theory posts | Walden fans post "prediction" threads with character tags [O] | Plant fair clues; reward theorists |
| "Justice for" posts | Fans campaign for side characters [I] | Give secondary characters their own goals and arcs |
| Comment quotes | Fans quote and react to specific lines (Walden commenters singled out comic exchanges) [P] | Write quotable lines that work out of context |
| "Fix-it" fics | Fans rewrite endings they disliked [K] | Endings that betray a character's logic generate backlash; earn every turn |

**Rule:** a signal is data about what fans *want more of*, not permission to reproduce their writing. Fanfiction is the fans' own creative work and is never copied.

### E2.7 The transformation test (originality and IP)
Before any concept moves forward, the engine answers all six. Any "no" sends it back.

1. Could a viewer who knows the source name it from our logline alone? (Must be **no**.)
2. Are all character names, relationship configurations, and backstories original?
3. Is the setting a Roblox-native world we built or have rights to, not a recreation of the source's world?
4. Does the plot sequence differ from the source beyond swapping names (different inciting incident, different midpoint, different ending)?
5. Are all songs, lyrics, and music original or licensed?
6. Does the concept combine at least **three mechanisms from at least two different shelves**? (Single-source concepts drift toward imitation; combinations produce originality.)

Adapting a specific title (a K-drama remake, for example) is a separate business decision that requires a license before any writing begins (Bible 12.4).

## E4. The relationship web

### E4.1 The web template
Every series fills these positions. One character can hold two, but no position may be empty. [I]

| Position | Function | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| **The Lead** | Wants something specific, has a useful skill, carries a secret | Passive, things happen to her |
| **The Pull** (love interest or core rival) | Wants something incompatible with the lead's goal | Exists only to react to the lead |
| **The Best Friend** | Has an independent goal that could take them away | Comments on the couple and nothing else |
| **The Rival** | Is right about one thing the lead won't admit | Pure villain |

| **The Keeper** | An adult, mentor, admin, or system that holds power | Useless or cartoonishly evil adult |

## E6. Structure: seasons, episodes, hooks, and cliffhangers

### E6.1 Long-form season map (10 episodes of 12 to 25 minutes)
Derived from K-drama pacing compressed to the 10 to 12 episode seasons K-dramas are moving toward, plus the Walden decay pattern. [D][O][I]

| Ep | Movement | Must happen | Relationship beat | Pull at the end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Collision | Status shock; world rule; the named ship and rivalry; the friend group; the season question | First meeting with friction | A decision the lead makes that we'll pay for |
| 2 | Collision | The cost of Ep 1's decision; the second lead enters | The first "almost" | A secret surfaces |
| 3 | Entanglement | Forced proximity begins; a side character's own goal appears | Grudging respect | Someone sees something they shouldn't |
| 4 | Entanglement | First pinch point: the antagonist or system strikes | A moment of care (hurt/comfort) | A betrayal by someone trusted |
| 5 | The Flip | The midpoint: first kiss, alliance, or major reveal; the story changes its question | The relationship becomes real | The reveal's consequences arrive |
| 6 | Reckoning | External pressure; the group splits | Happiness under threat | A forced choice between love and loyalty |
| 7 | Reckoning | The second lead's best case; the rival is proven right about one thing | Doubt | The worst secret is about to leak |
| 8 | Reckoning | The rupture (the late breakup or friendship crisis) | Separation | The lead makes the hardest choice |
| 9 | Release | The lead acts with everything she's learned | Repair begins | The confrontation is set |
| 10 | Release | The payoff of the season question, with a cost that stays | Earned reunion or earned ending | A new question opens Season 2 |

**Retention rules for long-form** [O][I]

- Episode 2 must start within seconds of where Episode 1 ended and pay off its cliffhanger in the first scene.
- Release on a fixed day and time. Every reference studio lost momentum to multi-week gaps.
- Each episode title is an idiom or phrase with a double meaning, in the reference format ("Poison Ivy," "Terms & Conditions," "Social Currency"). [O]

### E6.2 Vertical season map (40 chapters of 90 seconds to 3 minutes)

| Chapters | Purpose | Required beats |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 | Hook the stranger | Status reversal or shock in the first 3 seconds of Ch 1; the lead's want; the antagonist's face; a cliffhanger every chapter |
| 4 to 9 | Lock attachment | The ship or rivalry crystallizes; the first secret; one small win for the lead |
| 8 to 10 | **First major reversal** | The biggest turn so far lands here, where microdrama audiences decide whether to continue [D] |
| 11 to 20 | Escalate | Each cliffhanger bigger than the last; at least one reveal per 5 chapters |
| 20 | Midpoint flip | The relationship becomes real or the rule breaks |
| 21 to 32 | Reckoning | Consequences, the rival's best case, the late rupture around Ch 30 to 32 |
| 33 to 39 | Climb back | The lead acts; allies return |
| 40 | Release | Payoff plus a new question |

**Escalation rule:** "Episode 20 needs to matter more than episode 10." [D] Plot every cliffhanger on one list before scripting and check that intensity climbs.

### E6.3 Episode templates by length
**A. First-contact clip or micro chapter (60 to 90 seconds)** [D][I]

| Seconds | Beat | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 | HOOK | A face mid-reaction, a shock line, or a reveal in motion |
| 3 to 15 | PRESSURE | Who wants what; what blocks them |
| 15 to 45 | TURN | One reversal |
| 45 to 55 | PROOF | The peak: the confession, the reveal, the win, the scare |
| 55 to 60+ | PULL | Cut one beat before resolution |

**B. Short chapter (2 to 4 minutes)**: Hook (0 to 5s) · Pressure (to 0:40) · first Turn (around 1:15) · Proof (around 2:00) · second Turn or decision (around 2:45) · Pull.

**C. Long-form episode (12 to 25 minutes)**

| Section | Length | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Cold open | 30 to 90 seconds | The episode's most dramatic image, or a flash-forward; then rewind. The high-velocity Roblox tier uses flash-forwards to hold retention. [D] |
| Act 1 | ~25% | Pay off last episode's cliffhanger; set this episode's question; A plot (main relationship) and B plot (friend or side character with their own goal) |
| Act 2 | ~35% | Complications; the turn; a quiet scene (domestic or hurt/comfort) to let emotion breathe |
| Act 3 | ~30% | The proof: answer one question with a cost |
| Tag | Final 30 to 60 seconds | The pull: a new question, ideally a decision |

| ID | Hook formula | Example in a game world (original) | Best cohorts |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | **Status reversal flash-forward:** show her at the bottom, flash to the top (or the reverse) [D] | Her avatar in a crown on stage; cut to her, three weeks earlier, mopping the server's cafe | DG |

### E6.5 Cliffhanger taxonomy
Every Pull is one of these. Rotate types; never use the same type three chapters in a row. [D][I]

| Type | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reveal** | New information changes everything | The anonymous account posts from her best friend's device |
| **Threat** | Danger arrives | The player with no username is standing behind them |
| **Decision** | A character must choose, and we cut before the choice | Save the competition or save the friend |
| **Arrival** | Someone walks in | The ex-best friend joins the server |
| **Overheard** | A character hears what they weren't meant to | "She's only here because we needed a scholarship kid." |
| **Reversal of status** | Up becomes down or down becomes up | The winner is disqualified |
| **The almost** | A confession or kiss interrupted | A voice call connects just as he starts to speak |
| **Quiet dread** | No shock, just wrongness | Seven chairs; six players |

**Answer and reopen:** every chapter's cliffhanger is answered within the first 20% of the next chapter, and the answer opens a new question. Never resolve a cliffhanger off-screen.

### E6.6 The reveal ladder (for mysteries and secrets)

| Rung | What the audience knows | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Plant | A detail is visible but unremarked | Rewards rewatchers and theorists |
| 2. Notice | A character registers it | Signals importance |
| 3. Misread | Someone draws the wrong conclusion | Creates a red herring with a fair basis |
| 4. Confirm partially | Evidence narrows suspects | Keeps theorists active |
| 5. Reveal | The truth, with a cost | Payoff |
| 6. Recontextualize | An earlier scene now means something different | The edit-bait moment; drives rewatches |

Every reveal must have at least two earlier plants viewers could have caught.

### E6.7 Edit-bait engineering
Fans recut scenes into edits and screenshot frames as currency (Bible Sections 3.3 and 3.12). Design them on purpose. [O][I]

- **The held look:** a beat longer than comfortable, at the end of a scene, with no dialogue.
- **The callback frame:** a shot that mirrors an earlier shot with reversed meaning.
- **The quotable line:** under 12 words, works with zero context.
- **The outfit moment:** a transformation look revealed with a slow pan (and recreatable from the catalog: Bible 9.4).
- **The silent texture:** rain on a window, an LED-lit room, a crowd going quiet. Low-light atmospheric palettes are this audience's visual register. (Bible 9.3)
- **At least three edit-bait moments per long episode, one per vertical chapter.**

## E8. The game translation layer

### E8.1 Translating real-world tropes into game logic

| Real-world element | Game-native equivalent | Why it's better in a game |
|---|---|---|
| Social rank | Badges, VIP areas, private servers, follower counts, admin powers | Status is literally visible |
| A letter or note | A group-chat message, a sign in the world, a build | Evidence persists and can be screenshotted |
| A school | A roleplay school map, a conservatory, a guild | Familiar to the audience from Berry Avenue and Brookhaven |
| Moving away | Logging off for good, account deleted, a server shutdown | A loss the audience has felt |
| A disguise | An alt account, a changed username, a new avatar | Identity is fluid by design |
| Time passing | Server resets, seasonal events, map updates | Natural episode breaks |
| A ghost or entity | A player with no username, an NPC that remembers you, a glitch | Uniquely uncanny in this medium |
| A wedding, prom, or gala | An in-game event, a fashion show, a festival | Spectacle with built-in wardrobe moments |
| Money | Robux-style currency, rare items, limited skins | The audience knows the value |
| Exile | A ban, being kicked from the group, losing build permissions | Clear, visible consequences |

### E8.2 Native devices only this medium has
Respawn (death that isn't final, so what is?) · lag and freeze as tension · the chat bubble as dialogue and evidence · emotes as body language · the camera that one player controls · admin commands as power · build permissions as trust · the server list as a world map · AFK as absence · the leaderboard as status · voice chat on and off as intimacy · the avatar editor as transformation · a private server as a safe room. [I]

### E8.3 Staging rules (from the Bible)

- Decide the reality rule up front: characters know they're players, live fully in the fiction, or switch in a way the viewer can follow (Bible 10.10).
- Make decisive choices visible through blocking (who steps aside, who takes the last seat) since avatar faces have limited range.
- Facial animation matters to this audience; tutorials on dynamic heads draw hundreds of thousands of views. [O]
- Readable at phone size and with sound off (Bible 10.9, 10.13).

## E10. Quality gates and the learning loop

The complete audit, with thresholds, is Part G. This section covers the formula check and the learning loop.

### E10.1 Formula compliance check (run before the Bible's Resonance test)

| Check | Pass condition |
|---|---|
| Promise | The cohort's reason to come back is visible in every episode |
| Engine | Ten episode premises exist without new premises |
| Web | Every position filled; best friend has an independent goal; rival is right about one thing |
| World | At least one thing happens that could only happen in a game world |
| Cadence | Every episode has all five beats; cliffhanger types rotate; intensity climbs |
| Voice | Passes Bible 8.16; specificity quota met; zero AI-tell blacklist hits (E11.3) |
| Suspense | Information Ledger complete; Question Stack never below four; no fake-outs; reveals pass the surprising-and-inevitable test (E12.5) |
| Virality | Compelling Test 15+ of 20 (E13.6) |
| Originality | Passes the transformation test (E2.7) |
| Safety | Passes Bible Section 12 |

### E10.2 The learning loop: make the system smarter with every release

1. **Tag everything.** Every episode carries its mechanism IDs, hook type, cliffhanger type, engine, and cohort.
2. **Measure the right things.** For each episode: next-chapter selection per eligible viewer, voluntary return within a fixed window, completion, and shares (Bible B2 definitions). Public view totals are a discovery signal, not same-viewer retention.
3. **Correlate.** After every 10 episodes, rank mechanisms, hook types, and cliffhanger types by next-chapter selection within each cohort.
4. **Update the library.** Promote mechanisms that retain; flag ones that don't; add new cards from fan signals (E2.6).
5. **Retire stale language.** Refresh slang monthly (Bible 8.18).
6. **Never generalize from one show.** A mechanism needs results across at least three shows before it becomes a default.

## E11. The human voice layer: how to not sound like AI

This is the summary. The full training, with research on how this audience actually talks and texts and the line-by-line rewrite protocol, is Part D.

This section exists because formula alone produces shows that are correctly built and instantly forgettable. Real writers, whether a showrunner or a fanfiction author with 40,000 kudos, are specific, uneven, petty, funny, and occasionally wrong. Models default to the average. Every rule below pushes away from the average. [I]

### E11.1 The test that matters
Before anything ships, ask: **could only this show have this line, this detail, this scene?** If a line could be lifted into any other teen drama unchanged, it's generic. Rewrite it until it belongs here.

### E11.2 Specificity quota and the wrong-but-true detail

- **Quota:** every long episode has at least **eight specific, lived details**; every vertical chapter at least **two**. A specific detail names something only a person who'd been there would know.
- **The wrong-but-true detail:** at least one per episode should be a detail a model wouldn't predict but a real 17-year-old would instantly recognize.

| Generic (AI default) | Specific | Wrong-but-true |
|---|---|---|
| "the lunch table" | "the table by the vending machine that eats dollar bills" | "the table where you can charge your phone if you sit on the floor" |
| "her room" | "her room, LED strip half-dead on one side" | "her room, the LED strip stuck on the purple setting since 2024 because the remote's lost" |
| "a song they wrote together" | "the voice memo of them fake-crying to a sad song at 2am" | "the voice memo is 14 seconds long and one of them is laughing so hard it's mostly breathing" |
| "a keychain" | "the matching Labubu they bought, his is fake" | "she knew his was fake the whole time and never said" |
| "she logs off upset" | "she sets her status to invisible" | "she sets her status to invisible but forgets she's still in the voice channel" |
| "the group chat" | "the group chat named after an inside joke" | "the group chat that's been renamed eleven times; the current name is the last thing someone misspelled" |

**Where to get them:** the Bible's culture map (Section E9), texting conventions (E8.4), Roblox lexicon (E8.9), the youth reader panel (Bible B1), and real fan comments on reference shows. Never invent statistics, brands, or events that didn't happen; specificity comes from texture, not fabricated facts.

### E11.3 The AI-tell blacklist
Search every script for these. Each one is a strong sign a model wrote it. Rewrite on sight.

| AI tell | Example (including from our own earlier drafts) | What a human writes instead |
|---|---|---|
| **"It's not X. It's Y."** | "It's not that I changed. It's that I stopped asking for permission." | A choice that shows the change. She walks past the table she used to ask to sit at. |
| **"Both of those are true."** / balanced self-aware statements | "I love you. I'm not lying for you. Both of those are true." | "I'm not lying for you." Then she does it anyway, badly. Or she doesn't, and won't look at her. |
| **Quip button after emotion** | "That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me." / "Don't get used to it." / "Too late." | End on action or silence. He hands her the jacket. She doesn't put it on. She doesn't give it back. |
| **Characters narrate their feelings in full sentences** | "Because I wanted it. And because I wanted you to have it. Both. It's annoying." | "...whatever. congrats." (and the camera knows) |
| **Aphorisms and symmetrical phrasing** | "every look they build together is better than anything they made apart" | Say what literally happens: "they keep winning, and they hate it" |
| **Therapy-speak delivered sincerely** | "I need to protect my peace." (meant earnestly) | Use it as a deflection or a joke: "protecting my peace" said while blocking someone mid-argument |
| **Everyone is wise** | A 17-year-old explains her own trauma accurately | She gets it wrong. Her friend gets it right three episodes later. |
| **Threes** | "She was smart, kind, and brave." | One trait, shown |
| **Stock emotional props** | rain on the window at the sad moment, a song written together, a necklace from a dead grandmother | The prop should be funny, embarrassing, or weird before it's sad |
| **Default names** | Mira, June, Kai, Theo, Elara, Lyra, Ava, Luna, Zara, Dani | See 11.4 |
| **The polite villain monologue** | The antagonist explains her motives at length | The antagonist gets one line, and it's right |
| **Everyone gets closure** | Every arc resolves cleanly | Leave one thing unfixed. Real friendships end without a scene. |
| **"A beat."** overuse in scripts | *(beat)* before every line | Use pauses where silence is the point, maybe twice an episode |

### E11.4 Names, usernames, and display names

- **Use names real people born 2005 to 2010 actually have**, across backgrounds: Brielle, Destiny, Jaylen, Nayeli, Marisol, Kayleigh, Aaliyah, Jordyn, Isaiah, Bryce, Gianna, Trinity, Malik, Ximena, Hailey, Tristan, Anaya, Josiah. Avoid names that read as fantasy-novel or model defaults (E11.3). [I]
- **Every character has a username**, and fans will use it. Usernames are characterization: *lilbrieee_08* tells you who she was at 11; *xBryceOnTopx* tells you who he thinks he is; a username that's just a string of numbers is a mystery.
- **Display name changes are plot.** Changing a display name the day after a fight is a public statement the audience will read instantly.

### E11.5 The inarticulacy rule (the 20% rule)

- Characters say what they feel directly **at most 20% of the time**, and almost never in the first half of a season.
- The other 80%: they say something adjacent, joke, change the subject, go quiet, type and delete, or do something.
- When a character finally says the true thing plainly, it should cost them, and it should be short. "i miss you" beats a paragraph every time.

### E11.6 Mess, pettiness, and bad behavior that's fun to watch
This audience loves a character who is wrong in an entertaining way. Models avoid it because it reads as "negative." Put it back. [I]

- A lead can be petty, jealous, dramatic, unfair, and still be the lead. Let her unfollow someone at 2am and regret it at 2:01.
- Let friends be mean to each other in the way real friends are (affectionate roasts, Bible 8.5), and occasionally mean in the way real friends are not, with consequences.
- Comedy comes from the gap between how dignified a character thinks she's being and how she looks.
- The line stays where the Bible puts it: no cruelty about identity, no humiliation as the only joke, no rewarding abuse (Bible 12).

### E11.7 Roblox-native texture
Details that only someone who actually plays would write: the avatar that's clearly wearing a free starter item under a 400-Robux jacket; someone typing in chat during an emotional voice-chat moment because their mic is broken; the friend who always joins "1 sec" and arrives eleven minutes later; the server that kicks everyone at the worst moment; a dramatic exit undercut by the player clipping through a wall; the private server named something embarrassing from years ago. One per episode, minimum.

## E12. The suspense system

A cliffhanger only works if the viewer already cares what happens next. That caring comes from suspense: controlling who knows what, and making the audience dread a choice they can see coming. [K][I]

### E12.1 The Information Ledger: decide what the audience knows
For every major secret or threat, choose one of three modes, deliberately, and record it.

| Mode | Audience knows | Characters know | Feeling | When to use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Suspense** | The bomb is under the table | They don't | Dread, shouting at the screen | Most of the time. A clip of this travels because viewers want to warn the character. |
| **Mystery** | Something happened, not what | Someone on screen knows | Curiosity, theorizing | The season question; fuels theory videos |
| **Dramatic irony** | The truth | One character is wrong about it | Anticipation of the moment they find out | Ships, secret identities, fake relationships |

The classic illustration: two people talk at a table; a bomb goes off: 15 seconds of surprise. Show the bomb under the table first, and the same conversation becomes 5 minutes of suspense. [K]

| Information Ledger | Who knows | Audience knows? | Mode | When the audience learns | When the character learns |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Rule:** in any episode, at least one major piece of information is in **suspense** or **dramatic irony** mode. Pure mystery for a whole season exhausts viewers who can't solve it.

### E12.2 The Question Stack
At every moment, the viewer should be holding questions of different sizes. People are pulled back by unfinished business. [I]

| Size | Lifespan | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Season question** | The whole season | Who unfollowed her and why? |
| **Mid questions (2)** | 3 to 5 episodes | Is Jaylen lying about where he was? Will Destiny tell her mom? |
| **Episode question** | One episode | Will she sit at the table? |
| **Micro question** | One scene | What's in the screenshot she just got? |

**Rule:** close at least one question every episode (the PROOF beat), and open one new question at least as large as the one you closed. Never let the stack drop below four.

### E12.3 Causality: turns come from choices

- Every turn must trace to a **choice a character made on screen**, ideally one the audience understood and dreaded.
- Coincidence can get characters *into* trouble, never out of it. [K]
- **The "therefore/but" test:** connect each beat to the next with "therefore" or "but." If you can only connect them with "and then," the story is a list. [K]

### E12.4 The fake-out ban and cliffhanger debt

- **Banned:** cliffhangers resolved by "it was a dream," a misheard word, a character who was "just kidding," a phone that was someone else's, or any reveal that cheats what we were shown. This audience screenshots plot holes and posts them. One fake-out costs trust for the rest of the season.
- **Cliffhanger debt:** every cliffhanger is a promise. Pay it in the first 20% of the next chapter, and pay it *bigger or stranger* than expected. If the payoff is smaller than the setup, you owe the viewer, and they will collect by leaving.
- **Escalation honesty:** if Episode 10's cliffhanger was a betrayal, Episode 11's can't be a mild disagreement.

### E12.5 Surprising and inevitable
A great reveal makes the viewer think "I didn't see it coming" and then, instantly, "it was there the whole time."

- **Two plants minimum**, at least one hidden in plain sight in a scene that seemed to be about something else.
- **The rewatch frame:** after the reveal, show (or let fans find) the earlier moment that now means something different. This is the clip that goes viral as "the clue we all missed."
- **The misdirect:** give theorists a reasonable wrong answer with real evidence behind it. Fans love being wrong when the right answer is better.
- **Test:** explain the reveal to someone who watched every episode. If they say "wait, that doesn't make sense," it fails. If they say "oh no," it passes.

### E12.6 Stakes that a teenager actually feels
Abstract stakes (a name on a wall, a ranking, a permission level) don't land unless they're tied to something the audience has felt. Translate every stake into one of these, which the Bible's research says this audience lives with (Bible 9.2): being left out, being the last to know, being replaced in a friend group, being seen as fake, being publicly embarrassed, losing someone without a fight, being blamed for something you didn't do, being the one who did it and hiding it. [D][I]

### E12.7 Ticking clocks
Every season and most episodes have a deadline the audience can see: a tournament in three days, a screenshot that will be posted at midnight, a server shutting down Friday, a move-out date. Time pressure turns choices into dread.

## E13. The virality layer

Retention keeps a viewer inside a show. Virality is what carries the show to people who've never heard of it. Viral Roblox and teen shows travel through clips, arguments, ships, lines, and things people can recreate. Design each on purpose. [O][D][I]

### E13.1 The ten viral vectors

| # | Vector | What it is | Proof it works for this audience |
|---|---|---|---|
| V1 | **The context-free clip** | A 15 to 45 second moment that makes sense with zero setup and makes people feel something | stinkella's single-moment titles in the millions; fan edits of Walden [O] |
| V2 | **Debate bait** | A moral fork viewers will argue about in comments: was she right? | Revenge and betrayal titles dominate the reference channels [O] |
| V3 | **Ship war** | Two viable pairings; fans pick sides | Walden fans coined "Romaire"; second lead syndrome splits K-drama fandoms [O][D] |
| V4 | **The line** | A short, quotable line people repeat in their own lives | Fan captions quote and react to specific lines [O] |
| V5 | **The recreatable artifact** | Something fans can make: an outfit, a song, a dance, a POV format, a build | The "Valentina Tutorial" drew 404K views [O]; KPop Demon Hunters songs spread through TikTok dances [D] |
| V6 | **The theory hook** | A fair mystery viewers can solve and post predictions about | Walden fans post prediction threads [O] |
| V7 | **The villain fans love to hate** | A character people discuss, defend, and edit | Bible 3.7: the morally gray girlboss fans aren't supposed to like |
| V8 | **"This is literally us"** | A dynamic viewers tag their friends in | Shows are social objects; teens discuss them with friends [D] |
| V9 | **The craft flex** | A shot or sequence that makes people ask "how did they do that in Roblox?" | The format's most-viewed films are the most ambitious [D] |
| V10 | **Participation** | A way in: auditions, votes, extras, fan art features | Casting call videos drew up to 770K views [O] |

### E13.2 Debate bait: the moral fork
Give the central conflict **two defensible sides**. The comment section becomes the marketing.

- The lead does something wrong for a sympathetic reason (she leaks the screenshot to protect her friend).
- The "villain" is right about something important.
- **Comment-war test:** write the top comment for each side. If you can't make both convincing, the fork is fake.

### E13.3 The two-ship rule
For romance-capable shows (18+ leads for romance-led stories): build **two viable pairings** for the lead, or a romance and a friendship that compete for her loyalty.

- Each option offers a different future (the K-drama safe choice versus the risky, growth-demanding one) [D].
- Name each pairing's dynamic so fans can name the ship.
- Don't decide the winner early in public. Ship wars sustain engagement for the whole season.
- For under-18 casts, the war is between **friend groups or loyalties**, not romantic partners.

### E13.4 The recreatable artifact
Every show ships with at least one thing viewers can make themselves:

- **The look:** a signature outfit buildable in Roblox (connects to the Blueberry catalog, Bible 9.4).
- **The sound:** an original song, chant, or audio line usable in TikTok edits.
- **The format:** a POV setup fans can copy ("POV: you're the scholarship kid on day one").
- **The gesture:** a hand sign, emote, or ritual from the show.

### E13.5 Titles and thumbnails that survive a scroll

- **Series title:** short, ownable, slightly strange (one or two words). *Unfollowed* beats *The Academy of Secrets*.
- **Episode titles:** the idiom-with-a-double-meaning format works for prestige releases [O]; for clips, use a first-person situation ("I found out who unfollowed me").
- **Thumbnail:** one face mid-reaction plus one object that implies the twist. No more than three words of text.
- **Scroll test:** show the title and thumbnail for one second to someone in the cohort. Can they tell you what the show is about? Do they want to know what happens?

### E13.6 The Compelling Test (score before scripting and again on the finished show)
Score each vector 0 (absent), 1 (present but weak), or 2 (strong). Maximum 20.

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 15 to 20 | **Viral candidate.** Build it. |
| 11 to 14 | **Solid show, limited reach.** Strengthen the two weakest vectors before scripting. |
| 0 to 10 | **Rethink the premise.** |

This sits alongside the Bible's Resonance test: Resonance asks "will the right people stay?"; the Compelling Test asks "will they bring other people?" A show needs both.

## E14. How real writers work

### E14.1 The showrunner's toolkit
Techniques working TV writers use that the earlier sections only implied. [K]

| Tool | What it means | How to apply it here |
|---|---|---|
| **What's the show every week?** | A pilot must show the audience what a typical episode will feel like, not only the premise | Episode 1 contains the engine running once at full speed |
| **The promise of the premise** | The scenes the audience signed up for when they read the logline | List the five scenes the logline promises. All five must appear in the season, and at least one in Episode 1. |
| **The obligatory scene** | The confrontation the audience has been waiting for. Skipping it feels like theft. | Track it on the season map. Delay it, never deny it. |
| **Enter late, leave early** | Start a scene at the last possible moment, leave at the first | Cut every scene's first and last lines in revision; most get better |
| **Every scene turns** | A scene ends with a different value than it began (trust to suspicion, safe to exposed) | Label each scene's start and end value. If they match, cut or rewrite. |
| **Subtext** | Characters talk about one thing and mean another | In key scenes, write what they actually mean in the margin, then make sure no one says it |
| **The runner** | A small recurring bit across episodes (a joke, an object, a ritual) | One per show; let it change meaning once |
| **Set piece** | One big, memorable sequence per episode built for the clip | The prom, the tournament final, the server shutdown, the reveal at the fashion show |
| **Break story on cards** | Outline beats as separate cards and rearrange | The engine outlines in batches of ten episodes before scripting any |

### E14.2 The fanfiction writer's toolkit
Fanfiction authors are often closer to this audience than any studio: they write what fans crave, chapter by chapter, and read the comments. Their techniques, translated for screen. [K][I]

| Technique | What it does | Screen translation |
|---|---|---|
| **Yearning** | The wanting is more pleasurable than the having | Hold the payoff. Let characters notice each other in small ways (he remembers her order; she saves his seat) for episodes before anything is said. |
| **POV intimacy** | Readers live inside a character's head | Voiceover sparingly, the camera lingering on what she looks at, on-screen texts she types and deletes |
| **The small specific gesture** | Love shown through precise attention | He's the only one who noticed she changed her username back. He never mentions it. |
| **Hurt, then specific comfort** | The comfort is the payoff, and it must be as specific as the hurt | Not a hug. He fixes the build someone griefed, exactly how she had it, down to the wrong-colored block she liked. |
| **The protective line** | A character finally reveals how much they care through anger on someone's behalf ("who did this to you") | A friend who's been neutral all season goes quiet, then logs into the rival's server. Cut. |
| **Mutual pining** | Both want it, both think the other doesn't | Dramatic irony mode (E12.1): the audience sees both sides |
| **The "oh" moment** | A character realizes their own feelings, often late, often mid-sentence | Mid-argument, she stops. Camera holds. She leaves without finishing. |
| **Chapter-end comment bait** | Ending on a line readers have to react to | The last line of a chapter is the one fans will type back in comments |
| **Tags as promise** | Readers choose by tags and feel betrayed if the story doesn't deliver them | Our trope labels in descriptions (Bible 8.15) are tags. Deliver them. |
| **Fix-it energy** | Readers love stories that give characters the ending canon denied them | Give a side character the moment fans have been campaigning for |

**What we don't take from fanfiction:** explicit content, age-inappropriate dynamics, or anything tagged for adult readers. Only the emotional mechanics transfer (Section E16).

### E14.3 The pilot's first 60 seconds (where viral shows are won)

1. **0 to 3 seconds:** a face, a line, or a wrong detail that raises a question.
2. **By 15 seconds:** we know who she is by what she does, not what she says.
3. **By 30 seconds:** we know what she wants and what's in the way.
4. **By 45 seconds:** something happens she didn't choose.
5. **By 60 seconds:** she makes a choice we'd argue about.

If the first minute could be posted as a standalone clip and get comments, the pilot works.

## E15. Worked example: one concept through the system

**Stage 0 brief.** Primary: Lore Keepers. Secondary: Drama Girls. US viewer, 17, watches after rehearsal while messaging a friend. Promise: "She comes to find out what her friend group thinks she did, gets the gut-drop of being shut out on day one, and returns because the only person who'll tell her is the last person she trusts."

**Stage 1 mechanisms.** M46 being the last to know · M45 the receipts · M43 the moral fork · M49 the two-faced friend · M03 enemies to lovers (as a slow, deniable alliance; she's 17, so it stays at loaded looks and loyalty) · M48 status update as weapon. Shelves: A (Roblox drama), C (K-drama restraint), E (BookTok trope), F (fanfic hurt/comfort). Six mechanisms, four shelves.

**Stage 2 concept: *Unfollowed*.**
Brielle (17, *lilbrieee_08*) comes back from summer to find her whole friend group unfollowed her overnight. Nobody will tell her why. The only person still talking to her is Tristan (17, *tristnnn*), the boy she got banned from the server last year, and he says he knows exactly what she did.

- **Engine:** E3 The Secret, with E1 The Institution underneath (a creative academy map where your friend group is your status).
- **World rule:** everything is public. Follows, group chats, who sits where in the cafeteria map. The academy's anonymous confessions board posts at midnight.
- **Transformation test:** no nameable source; original world, characters, and plot. Pass.

**Stage 3 scores.**

## E16. Safety recap for the story engine

- Romance-led shows default to leads aged 18+. Romance between minor characters stays at crushes, longing, and an implied kiss at most (Bible 12.1).
- Never carry over content from adult sources (adult-rated TV, explicit fanfiction, mature webtoons). Only abstract structure transfers.
- Horror gets its power from rules and dread, never gore. No self-harm, eating disorders, sexual violence, or pregnancy among minor characters (Bible 12.2).
- Humiliation is never the only route to comedy, and coercion is never rewarded as romance.
- Nothing from fanfiction's adult-tagged content, and no age-inappropriate dynamics, even as "emotional mechanics" (Section E14.2).
- No real people, creators, or minors' likenesses. No copyrighted songs, characters, or plots (Section E2.7, Bible 12.4).

### V3.4 Why it's addictive (the psychology)

| Driver | How the format delivers it | Tag |
|---|---|---|
| **Status reversal ("face-slap" payoff)** | The underestimated hero is humiliated, then publicly proven superior; the cycle repeats every few episodes. Chinese duanju writers call the payoff *shuang* (the rush of satisfaction). | [K][O] |
| **Hidden information** | Hidden identities and secret heirs give the audience knowledge characters lack (dramatic irony) | [O] |
| **Variable reward** | A cliffhanger every 60 to 90 seconds; you never know which tap delivers the big payoff | [D][I] |
| **Wish fulfillment** | Wealth, power, being chosen, revenge without consequences | [O] |
| **Low commitment, high completion** | Each unit is tiny; "one more" is always cheap | [D] |
| **Bedtime comfort** | Predictable emotional rhythms at the end of the day | [D][I] |
| **Sincerity as a counter-trend** | Recent top performers include stories with a "more sincere emotional center" (kindness, loyalty) alongside revenge | [O] |

**The *shuang* cycle (the format's emotional engine)** [K][I]

1. **Suppression:** the hero is wronged, mocked, underestimated.
2. **Build:** the audience knows something the antagonists don't (a hidden identity, a skill, an ally).
3. **Release:** a public reversal; the antagonist is exposed or humiliated.
4. **New suppression:** a bigger antagonist or a fresh injustice arrives.

In adult microdrama this cycle runs every 3 to 8 episodes. For bumpp, keep the cycle and its satisfaction, but change what's at stake and how cruel the humiliation gets (Section V9.3).

## V7. Distribution and marketing science

### V7.1 The funnel

1. **Discovery:** the first episodes, or the most dramatic scenes, are posted to social feeds or bought as ads. [D]
2. **Conversion:** viewers who want more follow a link to a dedicated app. [D]
3. **Monetization:** coins per episode, weekly subscriptions, or rewarded ads. [D]
4. **Retention:** daily new titles, coupons, and check-in rewards bring users back. [D]

Commissioners use social platforms two ways: host full runs on YouTube and monetize with ads, or tease on TikTok or Instagram and drive viewers to paid apps. [D]

### V7.2 What the ad data says

- Paid apps depend heavily on paid user acquisition; ReelShort's reported revenue and acquisition spend have been described as close to equal in at least one industry analysis. [D] (Treat as an analyst claim.)

FreeReels led global downloads in Q1 2026; Melolo built an ad-funded audience across Southeast Asia; TikTok launched PineDrama free; *Screen Time* reached about 580M views free and ad-supported. ## V9. The bumpp translation: microdrama science for a 16 to 22 audience in Roblox

### V9.1 Adopt, adapt, reject

| Element | Decision | How |
|---|---|---|
| 3-second hook, one reversal, cliffhanger | **Adopt** | Every chapter (E1.2 and E6.3) |
| Payoff every episode | **Add** | Microdramas sometimes withhold; we pay something off every chapter (Story Engine PROOF beat) |
| 60 to 100 episodes | **Adapt** | 30 to 40 chapters of 60 to 120 seconds; total 45 to 75 minutes. *Screen Time* proved a short total runtime can win reach. [D][I] |
| Paywall at Episode 8 to 12 | **Adapt** | No paywall. Put the biggest turn at Chapters 8 to 10 anyway, because that is where casual viewers decide whether to binge. Release Chapters 1 to 10 together. |
| Shuang (status reversal) cycle | **Adopt, re-staked** | Teen status instead of wealth (V9.2); humiliation is never the only route to comedy (Bible 12) |
| Literal, status-twist titles | **Adapt** | Keep instant legibility; drop the Harlequin register (V9.4) |
| Billionaire, mafia, captive, secret-baby tropes | **Reject** | Adult, regressive, or wrong for minors and our brand |
| Werewolf and fated-mate romance | **Adapt carefully** | Supernatural roleplay is native to Roblox; romance leads 18+, no possession or coercion dynamics |
| Ads cut from peak scenes | **Adapt** | Organic clips on TikTok, Shorts, and Roblox Moments, cut from the edit-bait moments (E6.7, E13.1) |
| Paid acquisition at scale | **Reject for launch** | Fandom-led distribution (Bible: Discord, creator, campus seeding) |
| Bedtime release | **Adopt** | Drop at 7 to 9 pm local time in the primary market |
| Daily drops | **Adopt** | After the initial 10-chapter drop, release 2 to 3 chapters a day or a fixed daily slot |

## V10. Measurement science

### V10.1 The metrics that matter, in order

| Metric | Definition | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **3-second hold** | Share of viewers still watching at 3 seconds | Tests the cold open |
| **Chapter 1 completion** | Share who finish Chapter 1 | Tests the hook and the pressure |
| **Ch 1 to Ch 2 continuation** | Of Chapter 1 completers, share who start Chapter 2 | The single most important number |
| **Ch 10 survival** | Of Chapter 1 starters, share who reach Chapter 10 | Tests the paywall-peak equivalent |
| **Binge depth** | Average chapters per session | Microdrama's core behavior |
| **Next-day return** | Share who come back within 24 hours | Habit formation |
| **Share rate** | Shares per 1,000 views | Virality (E13) |
| **Comment rate and comment type** | Comments per 1,000 views; share that are debate, theory, or quote | Debate bait and theory hooks working |
| **Edit volume** | Fan edits within 7 days | Fandom formation |

### V10.2 Benchmarks we can cite

- Paying viewers on flagship titles: 96% to 98% still watching at 10 minutes, 88% to 95% at an hour (My Drama and My Muse). [D] These are paying, self-selected viewers; free viewers will retain lower.
- Paid conversion on a leading app: about 24% of users. [D] Not applicable to a free model, but a ceiling for what committed fandom looks like.
- Heavy users drive 83% to 94% of returning watch time. [D]
- Sessions and time per user rise over the first 30 days. [D]

No public benchmark exists for free teen Roblox microdrama. bumpp's first season sets the baseline. [I]

### V10.3 The testing protocol (how the platforms do it, adapted)

1. **Test hooks before full production.** Platforms test title-specific creatives before scaling (My Drama is described as running title-specific creative testing). [D] Post three different 15-second cold opens for the same premise as organic clips; produce the series whose hook wins.
2. **Read drop-off by chapter.** Find the chapter where continuation falls hardest; rewrite or re-cut the cliffhanger before it.
3. **Kill or double down at Chapter 10.** If Ch 10 survival is weak, the premise isn't working; stop. If it's strong, greenlight Season 2 development immediately.
4. **Tag everything** with Story Engine mechanism IDs, hook types, and cliffhanger types, and rank them by continuation within each cohort.

# Part G. The gates

## G. The gates: every audit a show must pass

A concept is gated once (Gate 1). A script is gated twice: once by the writer and once by an adversarial critic pass (G7), then audited by the detector and a human review (G9). A show ships only when it passes every gate.

### G0. Gate summary and thresholds

| Gate | Question | Instrument | Pass |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P3 Bankability** (concept) | Is it built on a spine audiences have already rewarded? | Bankability Score (P3) | 10+ of 12 for the launch slate; production risk never 0 |
| **G1 House style** | Is it A24 × K-drama × Berry Avenue × Brookhaven, with taste? | House Style Scorecard (G1) | 18+ of 24; no ingredient under 4; all taste rules pass |
| **G2 Resonance** | Would the primary cohort stay? | Cohort scorecard (G2) | 14+ of 20 for the primary cohort; no dealbreaker |
| **G3 Viral potential** | Will they bring other people? | Compelling Test (E13.6) | 15+ of 20 |
| **G4 Viewer actions** | Will they continue, like, comment, share, save? | Viewer Action Test (G4) | 9+ of 12 per episode; Continue must score 2 |
| **G5 Binge** | Is it worth bingeing? | Binge Test (G5) | 8 of 10 checks |
| **G6 Integrity** | Voice, suspense, originality, safety | E11, E12, E2.7, E16, Bible 12 | Zero failures |
| **G7 Double check** | Does it survive a hostile read? | Critic protocol (G7) | All critic findings fixed and re-scored |
| **G9 Script audit** | Does it sound real, and does every scene work? | Automated detector plus human audit (G9), with the craft check (C6) | Detector PASS; human audit 16+ of 20, no item under 3 |
| **G10 Stickiness** | Is it impossible to stop watching? | Stickiness Test (K6) and checker (K6b) | Every chapter 10+ of 12 (automated items 10 of 10); average 11+; no stretch over 20 seconds without a beat |

### G7. The double-check protocol (the critic pass)
After the writer scores a script, the engine runs a second, hostile pass as three critics, in order. Each must list its top five problems before scoring.

1. **The 17-year-old skeptic** (primary cohort): "Where would I cringe, scroll, or stop? Which line would a real person never say? Which moment is fake?" This critic runs the full D7 rewrite protocol.
2. **The showrunner:** "Where does a scene not turn? Where is a twist caused by coincidence? Where is the obligatory scene missing? Where is information mishandled?"
3. **The house-style editor:** "Where is this cheap? Where is A24, K-drama, Berry Avenue, or Brookhaven only decorative? Where did we explain what the audience already saw?"

Fix every finding, then re-score G1 to G6. Record both the first and final scores on the cover page.

| Prestige pacing in a microdrama world | Slow middles, quiet scenes, chapters opening on scene description, soft endings; the first ten scripts failed the stickiness test | Part K: vertical-first, the chapter clock, G10 |

### G9. The script audit: automated detector plus human review
Every script is audited twice: by the automated detector (fast, objective, catches cadence and rhythm problems) and by the human audit (taste, truth, and craft, which no detector can judge). A script ships only when both pass. The detector catches what an AI writer does by habit; the human audit catches what a detector can't hear. [I]

**Step 1. Run the automated detector.** It measures rhythm (D3b), AI cadence (D6), texting conventions (D4), and banned forms. Thresholds are house targets, calibrated against the youth panel over time.

| Metric | Pass range | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Short lines (five words or fewer) | 25 to 45% (long-form); 30 to 50% (vertical) | Below reads speechy; above reads stunted (D3b) |
| Median line length | 6 to 12 words | Real conversation sits here |
| Runs of 40+ words | 3+ per long episode. Vertical: 5+ conflict runs of 25 to 45 words per 10 chapters, none over 45 | Characters must sometimes go off; in vertical, briefly and in conflict |
| Longest streak of one-liners | 8 or fewer | Ping-pong detector |
| Stock replies in one scene ("Okay." "Yeah." "I know.") | 3 or fewer | Filler masquerading as dialogue |
| (beat) count | 3 or fewer | Pauses are earned |
| quietly / softly / barely / gently tags | 3 or fewer | Let the line carry it |
| Blacklisted AI patterns | 0 | E11.3, D6 |
| Default AI names | 0 | E11.4 |
| Em or en dashes | 0 | House style |
| Texts ending in a period | Only when the character is angry or cold on purpose | D4 |

**The detector (reference implementation).** The engine runs this on every script body before scoring. It is deliberately simple; extend the blacklist whenever a new AI habit is caught.

```python
"""bumpp script detector: AI-cadence, rhythm and texting checks for a single script body (markdown screenplay)."""
import re, statistics, sys

SKIP = ('INT.','EXT.','CUT','SMASH','TITLE','FLASHBACK','BACK TO','ON SCREEN','GAME','THEME','END','NO SCORE',
        'SCREEN','OLD','CHAPTER','CAMERA','SFX','ABOVE','COLD','INTERCUT','JUMP','DING','TOMORROW')
CUE = re.compile(r"^\**([A-Z][A-Z0-9 .'()\-/,]{1,50})\**\s*$")

BLACKLIST = {  # pattern: label
    r"\bIt'?s not (?:that )?[^.?!]{1,40}[.!] It'?s (?!not)": "It's not X. It's Y.",
    r"\b[Bb]oth of (?:those|these) are true": "Both of those are true",
    r"nicest thing you'?ve ever said": "stock romance quip",
    r"[Dd]on'?t get used to it": "stock quip button",
    r"\bthe way you do when\b": "narrator wisdom",
    r"\b(?:That|It) lands\b|\bWe see it land\b": "narrator interprets",
    r"\bEvery(?: single)? viewer\b": "narrator addresses audience",
    r"\bIt is not just\b": "narrator interprets",
    r"\b(?:almost|nearly) (?:smiles|laughs|cries)\. (?:Doesn't|Does not)\b": "fragment cadence",
    r"\bNot unkindly\b": "fragment cadence",
    r"\bsomething (?:shifts|changes) in (?:her|his|their) (?:face|eyes)\b": "AI stock description",
    r"\ba beat too long\b": "AI stock description",
    r"\bprotect my peace\b": "sincere therapy-speak",
}
AI_NAMES = r"\b(Mira|June|Kai|Theo|Elara|Lyra|Ava|Luna|Zara|Dani|Seraphina|Aria)\b"
TAGS = r"\((?:quietly|softly|barely|gently)\)|\*\((?:quietly|softly|barely|gently)[^)]*\)\*"

def speeches(lines):
    out, cur, who = [], None, None
    for raw in lines:
        t = raw.strip()
        m = CUE.match(t)
        if m and not m.group(1).startswith(SKIP) and len(m.group(1).split()) <= 7:
            if cur is not None: out.append((who, cur.strip()))
            cur, who = '', m.group(1).split(' (')[0]
            continue
        if cur is not None:
            if t == '':
                out.append((who, cur.strip())); cur = None; continue
            if re.match(r'^\*\(.*\)\*$', t): continue
            cur += ' ' + t
    if cur: out.append((who, cur.strip()))
    return [(w, s) for w, s in out if s]

def scenes(text):
    return [s for s in re.split(r'\n(?=(?:\*\*)?(?:INT\.|EXT\.|SCREEN RECORDING|OLD SCREEN|\*\*CHAPTER))', text) if s.strip()]

def analyze(text):
    lines = text.split('\n')
    sp = speeches(lines)
    words = [len(s.split()) for _, s in sp]
    stock = sum(1 for _, s in sp if re.sub(r'[^a-z ]', '', s.lower()).strip() in ('okay','yeah','i know','no','yes','hey','hi','what','sorry','ok','yeah okay'))
    # longest streak of consecutive one-liners (<=5 words)
    streak = best = 0
    for w in words:
        streak = streak + 1 if w <= 5 else 0
        best = max(best, streak)
    hits = []
    for pat, label in BLACKLIST.items():
        for m in re.finditer(pat, text): hits.append(label)
    names = re.findall(AI_NAMES, text)
    texts = re.findall(r'ON SCREEN[^:\n]*:\s*\*([^*\n]+)\*', text) + re.findall(r'^\*([a-z0-9_.💿 ]+: [^*]+)\*', text, re.M)
    cap_texts = [t for t in texts if re.search(r':\s*[A-Z][a-z]', t) and not re.search(r'LMAO|PLS|NO |OMG', t)]
    period_texts = [t for t in texts if t.rstrip().endswith('.') and not t.rstrip().endswith('...')]
    per_scene_stock = []
    for sc in scenes(text):
        ssp = speeches(sc.split('\n'))
        per_scene_stock.append(sum(1 for _, s in ssp if re.sub(r'[^a-z ]', '', s.lower()).strip() in ('okay','yeah','i know','ok')))
    return {
        'lines': len(words),
        'short_pct': round(100 * sum(1 for w in words if w <= 5) / max(1, len(words))),
        'median': statistics.median(words) if words else 0,
        'runs_40plus': sum(1 for w in words if w >= 40),
        'max_line': max(words) if words else 0,
        'one_liner_streak': best,
        'stock_replies': stock,
        'max_stock_in_scene': max(per_scene_stock) if per_scene_stock else 0,
        'ellipsis_lines': sum(1 for _, s in sp if s.startswith('...') or s.endswith('...')),
        'beats': text.count('(beat)'),
        'adverb_tags': len(re.findall(TAGS, text)),
        'blacklist_hits': hits,
        'ai_names': sorted(set(names)),
        'em_dashes': text.count('\u2014') + text.count('\u2013'),
        'texts_checked': len(texts),
        'texts_with_periods': len(period_texts),
    }

THRESHOLDS = {  # metric: (test, message)
    'short_pct': (lambda v, m: 25 <= v <= 45, 'short lines should be 25 to 45%'),
    'median': (lambda v, m: 6 <= v <= 12, 'median line should be 6 to 12 words'),
    'runs_40plus': (lambda v, m: v >= m.get('min_runs', 3), 'not enough runs of 40+ words'),
    'one_liner_streak': (lambda v, m: v <= 8, 'more than 8 consecutive one-liners'),
    'max_stock_in_scene': (lambda v, m: v <= 3, 'more than 3 stock replies in a scene'),
    'beats': (lambda v, m: v <= 3, 'too many (beat)s'),
    'adverb_tags': (lambda v, m: v <= 3, 'too many quietly/softly/barely tags'),
    'blacklist_hits': (lambda v, m: len(v) == 0, 'blacklisted AI pattern'),
    'ai_names': (lambda v, m: len(v) == 0, 'default AI name'),
    'em_dashes': (lambda v, m: v == 0, 'em or en dash'),
}

def report(text, min_runs=3):
    r = analyze(text)
    fails = [msg for k, (test, msg) in THRESHOLDS.items() if not test(r[k], {'min_runs': min_runs})]
    return r, fails

if __name__ == '__main__':
    r, fails = report(open(sys.argv[1]).read())
    print(r); print('FAIL' if fails else 'PASS', fails)
```

**Step 2. The human audit (score each 0 to 4; pass at 16+ of 20, no item under 3)**

| Item | What a 4 looks like |
|---|---|
| **Authenticity** | A 17-year-old reading it aloud would say every line the way it's written; no line sounds written |
| **Humor** | The laughs come from character and specifics, land at the end of the line, and at least one would get clipped (C3) |
| **Suspense** | Every scene carries a question or a threat the audience can see coming (C2, E12) |
| **Drama** | One confrontation turns, ends with a line that can't be unsaid, and both sides are right about something (C4) |
| **Hooks** | The first line is mid-thought, the opening hook and ending type are named and tried-and-true, and the last 10 seconds demand the next episode (C1) |

**Step 3. The critic pass (G7),** with the 17-year-old skeptic running D7 and the craft check (C6).

**Step 4. The youth panel** (D7 step 8): every line a reader marks as "I'd never say that" is rewritten, no matter what the detector says.

**The audit report** (attached to every script's cover page)
```
DETECTOR: short_pct · median · runs_40plus · one_liner_streak · max_stock_in_scene ·
          beats · adverb_tags · blacklist_hits · ai_names · em_dashes → PASS/FAIL
HUMAN AUDIT: authenticity /4 · humor /4 · suspense /4 · drama /4 · hooks /4 → /20
CRAFT CHECK (C6): opening hook · ending type · laughs · suspense tools · confrontation · bit · runs
CHANGES MADE AFTER AUDIT:
```

### G10. The Stickiness Test
Every vertical chapter is scored on the six items in K6 (hook, recap-resolve, friction, spikes, payoff in clip, button) and run through the checker in K6b. **Pass:** every chapter 10+ of 12, a launch-drop average of 11+, and no stretch longer than 20 seconds without a beat. The checker confirms the beats are present and on time; the human audit (G9) and our own retention data (K7) decide whether they work.

# Part S. Script standard

## S. Script standard

### S1. Format conventions for Roblox-filmed scripts

| Element | Convention |
|---|---|
| Scene heading | `INT.` or `EXT.`, location (and map style), time of day. Example: `EXT. MAPLE COURT (BROOKHAVEN-STYLE TOWN). LATE AFTERNOON.` |
| Character introduction | NAME (age, *username*) on first appearance, plus one specific detail |
| Action lines | Present tense, three lines or fewer per block, written for what the camera sees |
| Dialogue | Natural, interruptible; lowercase only in texts and chat |
| `ON SCREEN:` | Texts, chats, notifications, usernames, exactly as typed (Bible 8.4) |
| `GAME:` | In-game events: lag, join and leave messages, server kicks, UI sounds |
| `CAMERA:` | Only when the shot carries meaning (the held wide, the slow push, the fit reveal) |
| `THEME` / `SFX` | Score and sound cues; silence is written as `NO SCORE.` |
| `[CLIP]` | The 15 to 45 second segment intended for social discovery |
| `[EDIT]` | An edit-bait frame |
| `[PLANT]` | A clue or callback for later |
| `[M##]` | Mechanism IDs, at the end of the scene that uses them |
| Chapters (vertical) | `CHAPTER N · "title" · runtime`; every chapter is written on the clock with bold timestamp tags: `**[0:00 HOOK]**`, `**[0:00 RECAP-RESOLVE]**` (Ch 2+), `**[0:12 FRICTION]**`, `**[0:24 SPIKE]**`, `**[0:44 TURN]**`, `**[1:02 BUTTON]**`, and a closing FREEZE (K2) |
| Interruptions | Written with an ellipsis or a cut, never a dash |

